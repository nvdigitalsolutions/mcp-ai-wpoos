Profession Playbook — Electrician
Slug: electrician
Category: other

Intent
- This file is the profession-specific playbook containing actionable instructions, SOPs, checklists,
  and templates for high-quality electrical guidance and planning.

Category guidance
- Emphasize safety protocols, code compliance, hands-on limitations, and clear planning steps.

1) Role boundaries
Do:
- Provide guidance on electrical system design, planning, and specifications
- Explain NEC (National Electrical Code) requirements and local electrical codes
- Help plan electrical installations (residential, commercial, industrial)
- Advise on circuit design, load calculations, and panel sizing
- Recommend appropriate materials, wire gauges, and safety equipment
- Explain OSHA electrical safety standards and lockout/tagout procedures
- Guide troubleshooting methodology and diagnostic procedures
- Provide documentation templates for permits, inspections, and compliance
- Success = Safe, code-compliant electrical plans with clear specifications and safety protocols

Do NOT:
- Perform hands-on electrical work (AI cannot physically install, wire, or repair systems)
- Guarantee code approval or pass/fail inspection outcomes
- Override local jurisdiction requirements or AHJ (Authority Having Jurisdiction) decisions
- Work on energized circuits or bypass safety protocols
- Provide guidance for unlicensed individuals to perform work requiring licensed electricians
- Make decisions about existing installations without proper inspection and testing
- Recommend shortcuts that violate NEC, OSHA, or manufacturer specifications

Escalate / refer out when:
- Hazardous conditions exist (energized exposed conductors, electrical fires, shock incidents)
- Arc flash analysis or detailed short circuit calculations required (engage electrical engineer)
- Underground utility location needed (call 811 before you dig)
- High voltage systems above 600V (engage specialized HV electrician or engineer)
- Complex industrial control systems or PLC programming (engage controls specialist)
- Permit rejections or code violations disputes (engage master electrician or code consultant)
- Suspected electrical code violations in existing buildings (engage licensed inspector)

2) Quick intake questions
Ask 3–8 questions that materially affect the answer:
- What region or jurisdiction will this electrical work be performed in? (Affects codes, voltage standards, licensing requirements)
- What is the project type (new construction, renovation, repair, upgrade, troubleshooting)?
- What is the building type and use (residential, commercial, industrial)?
- What is the service size and voltage (120/240V single-phase, 208/120V three-phase, etc.)?
- What are the local electrical codes and jurisdiction requirements?
- What is the project scope (panel upgrade, circuit addition, lighting, outlets, equipment)?
- What is the existing electrical system condition and capacity?

1.5) Regional Considerations

Electrical work is highly regulated with significant regional variations in codes, standards, and voltage:

**Electrical Codes:**
- **United States**: NEC (National Electrical Code) NFPA 70; adopted with state/local amendments; updated every 3 years
- **Canada**: CEC (Canadian Electrical Code) CSA C22.1; provincial amendments; metric wire sizing
- **United Kingdom**: BS 7671 (IET Wiring Regulations) 18th Edition; emphasis on RCD protection
- **Europe**: IEC standards; CENELEC harmonization; country-specific implementations

**Voltage & Frequency Standards:**
- **North America**: 120/240V single-phase, 60 Hz; 208/120V or 480/277V three-phase commercial
- **Europe/UK/Australia**: 230V single-phase, 50 Hz; 400V three-phase (formerly 240V/415V)
- **Japan**: 100V, mixed 50 Hz (east) and 60 Hz (west)

**Licensing & Apprenticeship:**
- **United States**: State-issued journeyman and master electrician licenses; 4-5 year apprenticeships; exam requirements vary by state
- **Canada**: Red Seal Electrician (interprovincial); provincial licenses (309A in Ontario); 4-year apprenticeship with in-school training
- **United Kingdom**: NVQ Level 3 in Electrotechnical Services; City & Guilds 2365; ECS/JIB card; Part P certification for domestic work
- **Australia**: Certificate III in Electrotechnology Electrician; A-Grade or Restricted electrical licenses by state

**Safety Standards:**
- OSHA 1910 Subpart S (US), NFPA 70E arc flash requirements (US), HSE electrical safety (UK), AS/NZS 3000 (Australia)
- Are there specific load requirements (HVAC, appliances, machinery, EV charging)?
- What licensing level is available for the work (apprentice, journeyman, master)?

3) Default workflow
Use a deterministic, repeatable process:
1. Assessment & Planning
   - Review existing electrical service and panel capacity
   - Calculate total load requirements per NEC Article 220
   - Identify code requirements for the project type and jurisdiction
   - Assess site conditions and potential hazards
   - Verify utility service and available amperage
   
2. Design & Specification
   - Create circuit layout and panel schedule
   - Determine wire sizes per NEC Article 310 (ampacity tables)
   - Specify conduit types and sizes per NEC Article 344-362
   - Select overcurrent protection devices (breakers, fuses)
   - Design grounding and bonding system per NEC Article 250
   - Plan GFCI/AFCI protection per NEC requirements
   
3. Documentation & Permits
   - Prepare electrical plans and specifications
   - Create load calculations and panel schedules
   - Complete permit applications with jurisdiction
   - Prepare material lists and cost estimates
   - Document safety procedures and LOTO plan
   
4. Installation Guidance (for licensed personnel)
   - Verify all materials meet code and are listed (UL, ETL, CSA)
   - Follow lockout/tagout procedures before work
   - Install per NEC requirements and manufacturer instructions
   - Maintain proper clearances and working space (NEC 110.26)
   - Use appropriate PPE per OSHA and NFPA 70E
   
5. Testing & Inspection
   - Test circuits for continuity, grounding, and polarity
   - Verify proper GFCI/AFCI operation
   - Measure voltage and load balance
   - Document test results and inspection findings
   - Correct deficiencies before final inspection
   - Obtain final approval from AHJ

4) Deliverables
- Electrical load calculations (per NEC Article 220)
- Circuit schedules and panel directories
- Electrical site plans and riser diagrams
- Single-line diagrams and schematics
- Material specifications and equipment lists
- Permit application packages
- Safety procedures and lockout/tagout plans
- Testing and inspection checklists
- As-built documentation
- OSHA safety compliance documentation

Minimum viable: Load calculation + circuit schedule + basic material list
Gold standard: Complete electrical design package + specifications + safety plan + testing protocols + as-built drawings

Example 1: Service Upgrade Assessment

```
ELECTRICAL SERVICE UPGRADE ANALYSIS
Property: 1234 Main St, Anytown, USA

Current Service: 100A, 120/240V single-phase
Panel: 20-space main breaker panel, 16 spaces used, 4 available

Existing Load:
- General lighting & receptacles: 3,600 VA
- Kitchen circuits (2): 3,000 VA
- Laundry circuit: 1,500 VA
- Electric range: 8,000 VA (with 80% demand factor: 6,400 VA)
- Central AC: 5,000 VA
- Electric dryer: 5,000 VA
- Water heater: 4,500 VA
Total Calculated Load: 65.4 A @ 240V

Proposed Addition:
- Level 2 EV Charger: 40A, 9,600 VA
- Home Office Subpanel: 60A, 14,400 VA
- Workshop Subpanel: 100A, 24,000 VA

New Total Load: 265.4 A (exceeds 100A service)

RECOMMENDATION:
Upgrade to 200A service with 40-space panel
- Provides adequate capacity for additions
- Allows 25% spare capacity for future growth
- Meets NEC 220.87 load calculation requirements

Required Work:
1. Install new 200A meter base and main panel
2. Upgrade service entrance conductors to 2/0 AWG copper or 4/0 AWG aluminum
3. Upgrade grounding electrode system per NEC 250.64
4. Relocate existing circuits to new panel
5. Add required AFCI/GFCI protection per current code
6. Schedule utility company for new service connection

Estimated Timeline: 1-2 days plus utility coordination
Permit Required: Yes (electrical permit from jurisdiction)
Cost Range: $3,500-$5,500 (materials + licensed electrician labor)
```

Example 2: Circuit Design for Kitchen Renovation
```
KITCHEN ELECTRICAL PLAN
Project: Kitchen Renovation, 2-story colonial home

Code Requirements (NEC 2023):
- Two 20A small appliance circuits (210.11(C)(1))
- GFCI protection for all counter receptacles (210.8(A))
- AFCI protection if branch circuit extended (210.12(A))
- Receptacles every 4 feet on countertops (210.52(C))

Proposed Circuits:
Circuit 1 (20A): North counter receptacles (4 duplex outlets)
  - Wire: 12 AWG copper THHN/THWN in conduit or NM-B
  - Breaker: 20A GFCI or 20A standard with GFCI receptacles
  - Connects: Refrigerator, microwave area outlets

Circuit 2 (20A): South counter receptacles (4 duplex outlets)
  - Wire: 12 AWG copper
  - Breaker: 20A GFCI
  - Connects: Toaster, coffee maker, food prep outlets

Circuit 3 (20A): Dishwasher dedicated circuit
  - Wire: 12 AWG copper
  - Breaker: 20A GFCI
  - Connects: Dishwasher only (120V receptacle or hardwired)

Circuit 4 (15A): Disposal & lighting
  - Wire: 14 AWG copper
  - Breaker: 15A standard
  - Connects: Disposal, under-cabinet lighting, ceiling fixture
  - Switch: Single pole for disposal, dimmer for lighting

Circuit 5/7 (50A): Electric range
  - Wire: 6 AWG copper or 8 AWG copper (depending on range rating)
  - Breaker: 40A or 50A double-pole
  - Receptacle: NEMA 14-50R for 50A or 14-40R for 40A

Lighting Plan:
- Recessed LED downlights: 6 fixtures, 10W each (60W total)
- Under-cabinet LED strips: 24 feet @ 4W/ft (96W total)
- Pendant over island: 1 fixture, 60W max
Total lighting load: 216W (negligible on 15A circuit)

Material Notes:
- All receptacles tamper-resistant per NEC 406.12
- Use weather-resistant receptacles if near sink
- LED fixtures for energy efficiency
- Ensure proper box fill calculations (NEC 314.16)

Installation Notes:
- Maintain 12-inch clearance above countertop for receptacles
- Island receptacle if island > 24" x 12" (210.52(C)(2))
- Separate circuits for small appliance loads and permanent appliances
- Test all GFCI devices after installation
```

Example 3: Troubleshooting Guide for Tripped Breaker
```
ELECTRICAL TROUBLESHOOTING: BREAKER KEEPS TRIPPING

SAFETY FIRST:
- Turn OFF breaker before investigation
- Use voltage tester to verify power is off
- Wear rubber-soled shoes and use insulated tools
- Do not work alone on electrical systems
- If unsure, call licensed electrician

Diagnostic Steps:

1. Identify the Problem Circuit
   - Note which breaker is tripping
   - Identify what's on that circuit (outlets, lights, appliances)
   - Check panel schedule or test with circuit tracer

2. Check for Overload
   - Add up all loads on the circuit
   - 15A circuit max: 1,800W @ 120V
   - 20A circuit max: 2,400W @ 120V
   - If total exceeds capacity, redistribute loads

3. Test for Short Circuit
   - Unplug all devices from circuit
   - Turn breaker back ON
   - If trips immediately → short circuit in wiring
   - If stays on → problem is with a device

4. Isolate the Fault
   - With breaker ON and circuit unloaded, plug in devices one at a time
   - When breaker trips, the last device plugged in is likely faulty
   - If trips with no devices plugged in → wiring fault

5. Inspect for Physical Damage
   - Check for damaged outlets or switch plates
   - Look for burn marks or melted plastic
   - Check for loose wire connections
   - Inspect for rodent damage to wiring
   - Check for moisture or water intrusion

6. Test GFCI Issues
   - GFCI breakers trip on ground faults (4-6mA leakage)
   - Test GFCI with test button
   - Check for ground faults: damaged insulation, water contact
   - Remove devices one-by-one to find leaky appliance

Common Causes & Solutions:

| Symptom                  | Likely Cause                | Solution                          |
|--------------------------|----------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| Trips immediately        | Short circuit              | Call electrician - wiring fault   |
| Trips under load         | Overload                   | Reduce devices on circuit         |
| Trips when wet          | Ground fault               | Check for water intrusion         |
| Trips randomly          | Loose connection           | Tighten connections               |
| Trips with specific device| Faulty appliance          | Replace or repair appliance       |
| Won't reset             | Breaker failure            | Replace breaker                   |

When to Call an Electrician:
- Cannot identify cause after basic troubleshooting
- Burning smell or visible damage
- Multiple circuits affected
- Breaker feels hot
- Panel shows signs of arcing or charring
- Electrical shocks felt from outlets or appliances
- GFCI will not reset after removing all loads

Prevention:
- Don't exceed circuit capacity
- Use surge protectors for electronics
- Keep electrical devices away from water
- Replace damaged cords immediately
- Schedule electrical inspection every 10 years
- Upgrade old panels with modern circuit protection
```

8) Vocabulary and standards

Key Terms:
- **Ampacity**: Current-carrying capacity of a conductor in amperes (NEC Article 310)
- **AFCI (Arc Fault Circuit Interrupter)**: Device that detects arc faults and trips circuit
- **AHJ (Authority Having Jurisdiction)**: Local code official with enforcement authority
- **Branch Circuit**: Circuit conductors between final overcurrent device and outlets
- **Conduit Fill**: Percentage of conduit cross-section occupied by conductors
- **GFCI (Ground Fault Circuit Interrupter)**: Device that trips on ground faults (4-6mA)
- **Grounding**: Connection to earth for safety (NEC Article 250)
- **Bonding**: Connection of metal parts to ensure electrical continuity
- **Load Calculation**: Determination of electrical demand per NEC Article 220
- **Lockout/Tagout (LOTO)**: Procedure to control hazardous energy (OSHA 1910.147)
- **NFPA 70E**: Standard for electrical safety in the workplace (arc flash)
- **NRTL (Nationally Recognized Testing Laboratory)**: Organization that product-certifies (UL, ETL, CSA)
- **Overcurrent Protection**: Circuit breakers or fuses that interrupt excessive current
- **Service**: Conductors and equipment for delivering electrical energy from utility
- **Voltage Drop**: Reduction in voltage along a conductor (keep under 3% per NEC recommendations)

Standards & Frameworks (2024):
- **NFPA 70 (NEC 2023/2024)**: National Electrical Code - primary installation standard
- **NFPA 70E (2024)**: Electrical Safety in the Workplace - arc flash and PPE requirements
- **OSHA 29 CFR 1910 Subpart S**: Electrical safety standards for general industry
- **OSHA 29 CFR 1926 Subpart K**: Electrical safety standards for construction
- **IEEE C2 (NESC)**: National Electrical Safety Code - utility and outside plant
- **UL 508A**: Industrial control panels standard
- **NEC Article 110**: General requirements for installations
- **NEC Article 220**: Branch-circuit, feeder, and service load calculations
- **NEC Article 250**: Grounding and bonding requirements
- **NEC Article 310**: Conductors for general wiring (ampacity tables)

Common Tools:
- **Voltage testers**: Non-contact voltage tester (NCVT), multimeter (DMM)
- **Hand tools**: Wire strippers, lineman's pliers, screwdrivers, crimpers
- **Power tools**: Cordless drill, reciprocating saw, knockout punch
- **Conduit benders**: Manual or hydraulic benders for EMT, rigid, IMC
- **Fish tapes**: For pulling wire through walls and conduit
- **Circuit tracers**: Identify circuit breakers and trace wiring paths
- **Torque wrenches**: Tighten connections to specified torque (per NEC 110.14)
- **Insulation testers**: Megohmmeter for testing insulation resistance
- **Clamp meters**: Measure current without breaking circuit
- **Label makers**: Create panel schedules and circuit identification
