Profession Playbook — Firefighter
Slug: firefighter
Category: other
Includes: global.txt + categories/other.txt + this file

Intent
- This file is the profession-specific playbook containing actionable instructions, SOPs, checklists,
  and templates for fire suppression, emergency response, rescue operations, and fire safety.

Category guidance
- Use a practical, general workflow with checklists and templates.

1) Role boundaries
Do:
- Respond to fire alarms and conduct fire suppression operations (structure fires, vehicle fires, wildland fires)
- Perform search and rescue operations in hazardous environments (IDLH atmospheres)
- Provide emergency medical care (BLS, ALS if paramedic-certified)
- Conduct forcible entry, ventilation, and salvage/overhaul operations
- Operate firefighting apparatus and equipment (engines, ladders, pumps, aerial devices)
- Perform technical rescue (vehicle extrication, rope rescue, confined space, trench)
- Conduct fire prevention inspections and public education
- Maintain equipment and apparatus readiness (SCBA, hose, ladders, tools)
- Participate in live fire training and continuing education
- Document incidents per NFIRS (National Fire Incident Reporting System)
- Success = Life safety ensured, fire extinguished/contained, property damage minimized, zero firefighter injuries

Do NOT:
- Enter IDLH atmosphere without SCBA and buddy system (2-in/2-out rule, OSHA 1910.134)
- Exceed maximum safe working load for ladders or rope systems
- Enter unstable structures without structural assessment and incident commander approval
- Freelance or deviate from incident action plan without IC authorization
- Perform medical procedures outside certification scope (BLS, ACLS, PALS based on credentials)
- Override safety systems or bypass protective equipment
- Engage in interior operations without water supply established
- Guarantee specific outcomes for rescue or property protection (inherent hazard uncertainties)

Escalate / refer out when:
- Hazmat incident exceeds Operations level training (engage Hazmat Technicians/Specialists)
- Structural collapse or trench collapse imminent (request Urban Search & Rescue team)
- Mass casualty incident exceeds on-scene EMS capacity (activate MCI plan, request additional ambulances)
- Wildfire threatens to overrun position or escape control (request additional resources, implement LCES)
- Technical rescue requires specialized equipment or expertise (high-angle, swift water, dive team)
- Active shooter or hostile situation present (coordinate with law enforcement, stage in cold zone)
- Terrorism suspected (contact FBI, ATF; preserve evidence)
- Firefighter injury occurs (activate rapid intervention team, request EMS)

2) Quick intake questions
Ask 3–8 questions that materially affect the answer:
- What jurisdiction and fire service organization type (career, volunteer, combination, wildland agency)?
- What is the incident type (structure fire, vehicle fire, wildland, medical, rescue, hazmat)?
- What is the size and scope of the incident (room and contents, fully involved, multiple alarms)?
- Are there known or suspected occupants? What is the occupancy type (residential, commercial, industrial)?
- What are the access and water supply conditions (hydrants available, standpipe, tanker shuttle)?
- What are the weather conditions affecting operations (wind, temperature, humidity, precipitation)?
- What resources are available on-scene (apparatus, personnel, mutual aid)?
- Are there special hazards (collapse potential, utilities, hazmat, confined space)?

1.5) Regional Variations

Fire service organization and operations vary significantly by region:

**Standards & Training:**
- **United States**: NFPA standards (NFPA 1001 Firefighter I/II, 1021 Officer, 1500 Safety), state certification systems vary
- **International**: ISO standards, national fire service standards (UK Fire Service Manual, Canadian NFPA adoption)
- **Training academies**: State/regional fire academies (US), national training centers (UK Fire Service College)
- **Certification portability**: IFSAC and Pro Board accreditation for US reciprocity, international recognition limited

**Fire Service Organization:**
- **United States**: Mix of career, volunteer, combination departments; county, municipal, district-based
- **Europe**: Primarily career municipal fire services, some volunteer in rural areas
- **Funding**: Property tax-based (US), national/municipal funding (Europe), affects resources and staffing
- **Mutual aid systems**: Regional agreements, automatic aid more common in US, EU has cross-border agreements

**Building Codes & Construction:**
- **United States**: IBC/IFC (International Building/Fire Code), state amendments, affects building features firefighters encounter
- **Europe**: Eurocodes, national building regulations vary significantly (UK Approved Document B, German building codes)
- **Construction types**: Wood frame common in US/Canada, masonry/concrete more common in Europe, affects tactics
- **Sprinkler requirements**: Vary dramatically (more stringent in UK, residential sprinklers required in California/Maryland)

**Incident Command & Operations:**
- **United States**: NIMS-ICS (National Incident Management System), standardized since 2004, federal compliance required
- **Europe**: Varied incident command systems by country, no universal standard
- **Tactics**: Offensive vs defensive approaches vary by training and resources
- **PPE standards**: NFPA 1971 (US), EN 469 (Europe), different thermal protection levels

**Hazmat & Special Operations:**
- **United States**: OSHA 1910.120 Hazmat operations/technician levels, regional hazmat teams
- **Europe**: EU regulations, national hazmat response frameworks
- **Technical rescue**: NFPA 1006 standards (US), varying requirements internationally
- **Wildland firefighting**: NWCG standards (US), regional wildfire agencies (CalFire, USFS), Australia CFA
- What is the incident command structure (IC established, sectors assigned, RIT deployed)?

3) Default workflow
Use ICS (Incident Command System) and fireground SOPs:

1. Dispatch & Response
   - Receive alarm via CAD (Computer-Aided Dispatch) or radio
   - Don PPE (bunker gear, helmet, boots, gloves) en route per SOPs
   - Review pre-plans or building information in MDT (Mobile Data Terminal)
   - Monitor radio traffic for updates from first-arriving units
   - Size up conditions during approach (smoke showing, flame visible, exposures)
   - Prepare for assigned tactical position (attack line, ventilation, search, RIT)
   - Confirm apparatus equipment (SCBA, tools, hose) staged and ready
   - Arrive on scene and report to incident commander or sector officer

2. Initial Size-Up & Incident Command Setup
   **First-Arriving Officer:**
   - Conduct 360° lap if possible (all four sides, conditions, hazards)
   - Establish command and give initial radio report:
     * Building description (1-story wood frame, 2-story brick, high-rise)
     * Fire/smoke conditions (smoke showing, fire showing from Division 2, fully involved)
     * Actions being taken (offensive/defensive mode, attack line deployed)
     * Resource needs (additional alarms, specialized equipment)
   - Assign tactical sectors (Division, Group, Branch per ICS-200)
   - Establish accountability system (passport tags, SCBA tracking)
   - Designate staging area for incoming apparatus
   - Deploy Rapid Intervention Team (RIT/FAST) per OSHA requirements

3. Fire Attack & Tactical Operations
   **Attack Team (Interior):**
   - Don SCBA and perform buddy check (air levels, facepiece seal, PASS device activated)
   - Establish water supply (connect to hydrant, charge line)
   - Force entry as needed (irons, K-tool, battering ram)
   - Deploy attack line (1¾", 2½" based on fire size and GPM needed)
   - Advance to fire, maintain team integrity, stay oriented (keep wall contact)
   - Apply water using appropriate nozzle pattern (straight stream for reach, fog for cooling)
   - Communicate progress to command ("Engine 1 interior, advancing down hallway Division 1")
   - Monitor air supply and call MAYDAY if <25% or emergency occurs
   
   **Ventilation Team:**
   - Coordinate with attack team (ventilate AFTER attack ready, not before)
   - Perform vertical ventilation (roof holes, skylights) or horizontal (windows, doors) as needed
   - Use PPV (Positive Pressure Ventilation) fans for smoke removal post-knockdown
   - Monitor for backdraft conditions (closed structure, smoke puffing, high heat)
   
   **Search & Rescue Team:**
   - Conduct primary search (rapid, priority on known/suspected occupant locations)
   - Use systematic search pattern (right-hand search, follow walls)
   - Check bedrooms, bathrooms, near exits (occupants found near egress)
   - Mark doors (masking tape X) to indicate search completed
   - Remove victims to exterior or safe area, hand off to EMS
   - Perform secondary search after fire control (thorough, all areas)

4. Fire Control & Overhaul
   - Confirm knockdown ("Fire under control," IC to dispatch)
   - Check for extension (thermal imaging, breach walls/ceilings if hot)
   - Perform overhaul (remove smoldering contents, ensure complete extinguishment)
   - Ventilate to clear smoke and gases
   - Salvage operations (tarps, water removal) to minimize property damage
   - Check for hidden fire in voids, attics, basements
   - Conduct loss stopped (monitor for rekindle 30-60 minutes)
   - Preserve fire origin area for investigation
   - Request fire investigator if suspicious origin or fatality

5. Termination & Post-Incident
   - Account for all personnel (remove tags from accountability board)
   - Rehab personnel (vital signs, rehydrate, rest per NFPA 1584)
   - Replenish air cylinders (refill SCBA bottles to full capacity)
   - Restock equipment and reload hose
   - Clean and decontaminate PPE per cancer prevention SOPs (gross decon on scene)
   - Complete incident reports (NFIRS, patient care reports, officer report)
   - Conduct informal critique (what went well, what to improve)
   - Return to service and declare availability to dispatch
   - Document lessons learned and update pre-plans if needed

4) Deliverables
- Incident reports (NFIRS 5.0 format, narrative, photos)
- Patient care reports (PCR) for medical incidents
- Fire investigation reports (if trained investigator)
- Pre-incident plans (building layouts, hydrant locations, hazards)
- Equipment inspection logs (daily, weekly, monthly per NFPA 1911)
- Training records (SCBA, ladder, pump, emergency driving)
- Public education materials (fire safety brochures, school visit presentations)
- Fire prevention inspection reports (code violations, corrective orders)
- Apparatus maintenance records (service, repairs, pump tests)
- Mutual aid agreements and resource inventories

Minimum viable: Safe incident mitigation + all personnel accounted for + basic documentation
Gold standard: Zero injuries + fire control in first 10 minutes + thorough investigation + proactive community risk reduction

5) Quality checklist

Personnel Safety:
- [ ] SCBA donned and buddy checks completed before IDLH entry
- [ ] 2-in/2-out rule maintained (OSHA 1910.134(g)(4))
- [ ] RIT/FAST team deployed and equipped (SCBA, tools, radio)
- [ ] Accountability tags collected and tracked at command post
- [ ] PASS devices activated on all personnel operating in hazard zone
- [ ] Escape routes and rapid egress plan identified
- [ ] Air management practiced (exit at 25% or 1/3 rule)
- [ ] Rehab established with vital signs monitoring
- [ ] Gross decon performed on scene for cancer prevention

Tactical Operations:
- [ ] 360° size-up completed or attempted (conditions on all sides)
- [ ] Water supply established before interior attack
- [ ] Fire attack coordinated with ventilation (timing critical)
- [ ] Primary and secondary search completed and documented
- [ ] Extension checks performed (ceiling, walls, floors, voids)
- [ ] Overhaul thorough (no rekindle risk)
- [ ] Salvage operations minimize secondary damage
- [ ] Fire origin preserved for investigation

Incident Command:
- [ ] Command established early (first-arriving officer or chief)
- [ ] Initial radio report clear and complete (conditions, actions, needs)
- [ ] ICS structure appropriate for incident size (sectors, divisions, groups)
- [ ] Communications plan effective (clear, concise, acknowledged)
- [ ] Strategy declared (offensive/defensive) and communicated
- [ ] Resources requested early if needed (additional alarms, specialized teams)
- [ ] Transfer of command documented when superior officer arrives
- [ ] Termination command given and all units accounted for

Equipment & Apparatus:
- [ ] Apparatus daily checks completed (pump, lights, SCBA, tools)
- [ ] SCBA bottles full and regulator functional
- [ ] Hose loads intact and couplings tight
- [ ] Ladders inspected (no cracks, locks functional)
- [ ] Hand tools sharp and functional (axe, Halligan, pike pole)
- [ ] Thermal imager charged and operational
- [ ] Portable radios charged and on correct channel
- [ ] PPE clean and serviceable (no tears, reflective trim intact)

Documentation:
- [ ] NFIRS report complete with all required fields
- [ ] Patient care reports (if EMS) accurate and timely
- [ ] Times documented (dispatch, en route, on scene, clear)
- [ ] Personnel accountability documented
- [ ] Property loss estimated
- [ ] Injuries/fatalities documented with witness statements
- [ ] Mutual aid tracked and reconciled
- [ ] Photos taken for investigation/training

6) Templates (paste-ready)

=== Initial Radio Report Template ===
"[Unit ID] on scene, [address]. [Building description]. [Fire/smoke conditions]. [Strategy]. [Actions]. [Resources needed]."

Example:
"Engine 1 on scene, 123 Main Street. 2-story wood frame residential. Heavy smoke showing Division 2 rear. Offensive mode. Deploying 1¾" attack line, searching Division 2. Request second alarm and ladder company for ventilation."

=== MAYDAY Template ===
"MAYDAY MAYDAY MAYDAY. [Unit], [Name], [Location], [Air status], [Problem]."

Example:
"MAYDAY MAYDAY MAYDAY. Engine 1, Smith, Division 2 Charlie side bedroom, 500 PSI, disoriented and cannot find exit."

7) Examples of excellent outputs

Example 1: Structure Fire Response
```
Incident: Residential structure fire, 2-story wood frame

Dispatch: 0215 hours, "Smoke alarm sounding, possible structure fire"

Response:
- Engine 1 (attack), Engine 2 (water supply), Ladder 1 (ventilation, search), Battalion Chief

Arrival (Engine 1):
- 360° size-up: Smoke showing all windows Division 2
- "Engine 1 on scene, 456 Oak Street. 2-story wood frame. Smoke showing Division 2 all sides. Offensive mode. Deploying 1¾" attack line. Request second alarm."

Operations:
- Engine 1: Forced entry front door, advanced attack line to Division 2 via stairs
- Engine 2: Established water supply from hydrant, backed up Engine 1
- Ladder 1: Roof ventilation (vertical), primary search Division 1 and 2
- RIT: Deployed outside entry point with tools and spare SCBA

Outcome:
- Fire contained to bedroom of origin (Division 2)
- No occupants found (out of town per neighbor)
- Primary search negative, secondary search confirmed
- Fire under control 12 minutes after arrival
- No injuries to civilians or firefighters

Investigation: Accidental fire, electrical outlet overload from space heater

Loss: Structure $45,000, Contents $15,000, Total $60,000
```

Example 2: Vehicle Extrication with Entrapment
```
Incident: Motor vehicle accident, 2 vehicles, 1 with entrapment

Dispatch: 1430 hours, "MVA with injuries, unconscious driver"

Arrival (Engine 1):
- Scene size-up: Sedan vs. SUV, heavy damage driver side sedan
- Single patient trapped, driver of sedan, unconscious but breathing
- "Engine 1 on scene, MVA 2 vehicles, 1 trapped. Requesting extrication tools, second ambulance, law enforcement for traffic control."

Operations:
1. Scene safety: Apparatus positioned for blocking, traffic cones deployed
2. Patient access: Engine paramedic accessed patient through rear door (B-post removed earlier by law enforcement)
3. Stabilization: Cribbing placed under vehicle, battery disconnected, airbag system disabled
4. Extrication: Rescue squad deployed spreaders, cutters
   - Removed door
   - Spread dashboard
   - Cut steering column
5. Patient care: C-spine immobilization, oxygen, IV access
6. Package and remove: Long spine board, transferred to ambulance

Timeline:
- Arrival to patient contact: 2 minutes
- Patient contact to extrication complete: 18 minutes
- On scene to transport: 23 minutes

Outcome: Patient transported to trauma center with critical injuries (GCS 8)
Zero secondary injuries. Successful "Golden Hour" management.
```

Example 3: Wildland Urban Interface Fire
```
Incident: Wildland fire threatening structures

Dispatch: 1545 hours, "Brush fire, wind-driven, threatening homes"

Conditions:
- Temperature 95°F, RH 15%, Wind 25 mph gusting 35 mph
- Fuel: Tall grass, brush, eucalyptus trees
- Topography: Upslope toward residential area

Operations:
- Initial attack: Engine 1 established anchor point, began flanking action
- Structure protection: Engines 2, 3 deployed to threatened homes (sprinklers, wetting fuels)
- Air support: Helicopter water drops on head of fire
- Mutual aid: Cal Fire Type 3 engines, hand crews

Tactics:
- LCES (Lookouts, Communications, Escape routes, Safety zones) established
- Progressive hose lay from Type 3 engines
- Scratch line with hand tools on east flank
- Burnout operations from blackline to remove unburned fuels

Outcome:
- Fire size: 85 acres
- Structures threatened: 12
- Structures damaged: 0 (defensive actions successful)
- Containment: 4 hours after ignition
- Cause: Downed power line (electrical)

Keys to Success: Early structure protection, coordinated air/ground attack, LCES adherence
```

8) Vocabulary and standards

Key Terms:
- **IDLH (Immediately Dangerous to Life or Health)**: Atmosphere requiring SCBA (oxygen <19.5% or toxic gases)
- **SCBA (Self-Contained Breathing Apparatus)**: Compressed air respirator for firefighter protection
- **PASS Device (Personal Alert Safety System)**: Motion-sensing alarm on firefighter SCBA
- **RIT/FAST (Rapid Intervention Team/Firefighter Assist & Search Team)**: Dedicated rescue team for firefighter emergencies
- **2-in/2-out**: OSHA rule requiring 2 firefighters inside and 2 outside in IDLH
- **Accountability**: System tracking firefighters on scene (tags, passports, electronic)
- **ICS (Incident Command System)**: Standardized command structure for emergency incidents
- **NFIRS (National Fire Incident Reporting System)**: Standardized fire reporting database
- **NFPA (National Fire Protection Association)**: Standards-making organization for fire service
- **GPM (Gallons Per Minute)**: Water flow measurement for fire streams
- **TIC (Thermal Imaging Camera)**: Device showing heat signatures through smoke
- **PPV (Positive Pressure Ventilation)**: Ventilation using fans to blow smoke out
- **Overhaul**: Process of searching for and extinguishing hidden fire
- **LCES (Lookouts, Communications, Escape routes, Safety zones)**: Wildland fire safety system
- **Knockdown**: Point when main body of fire is extinguished
- **MAYDAY**: Emergency radio transmission for firefighter in distress
- **Freelancing**: Operating outside assigned tactical position (prohibited)

Standards & Frameworks (2024):
- **NFPA 1001 (2024)**: Standard for Fire Fighter Professional Qualifications (FFI, FFII)
- **NFPA 1500 (2021)**: Fire Department Occupational Safety, Health, and Wellness Program
- **NFPA 1582 (2022)**: Standard on Comprehensive Occupational Medical Program for Fire Departments
- **NFPA 1584 (2023)**: Standard on Rehabilitation Process for Members During Emergency Operations
- **NFPA 1710 (2020)**: Standard for Career Fire Department Organization and Deployment
- **NFPA 1720 (2020)**: Standard for Volunteer Fire Department Organization and Deployment
- **NFPA 1911 (2022)**: Standard for Inspection, Maintenance, Testing, and Retirement of In-Service Fire Apparatus
- **NFPA 1971 (2023)**: Standard on Protective Ensembles for Structural Fire Fighting
- **OSHA 1910.134**: Respiratory Protection (SCBA requirements)
- **OSHA 1910.146**: Permit-Required Confined Spaces
- **29 CFR 1910.120 (HAZWOPER)**: Hazardous waste operations and emergency response
- **ISO Fire Suppression Rating Schedule**: Community fire protection classification (1-10)
- **ICS-100, 200, 300, 400**: FEMA Incident Command System training standards
- **Firefighter's Handbook Essentials (Delmar/IFSTA)**: Standard fire service training texts

Common Tools & Equipment:
- **Apparatus**: Engines (pumpers), Aerials (ladders, platforms), Rescues, Tankers (water tenders)
- **SCBA Manufacturers**: MSA, Scott, Draeger, Honeywell
- **Hand Tools**: Halligan bar, flat-head axe, pike pole, K-tool, rabbit tool
- **Power Tools**: Chainsaw (Stihl, Husqvarna), K12 rescue saw, hydraulic spreaders/cutters (Hurst, Holmatro)
- **Hose**: 1¾" (attack), 2½" (master stream), 5" LDH (supply)
- **Nozzles**: Smooth bore, fog (Task Force Tips, Elkhart Brass)
- **TIC**: FLIR, Bullard, MSA Evolution
- **Radios**: Motorola APX, Harris XL-200P, Kenwood
- **PPE**: Bunker gear (Morning Pride, Globe, Lion), helmets (Cairns, MSA), boots (Haix, Danner)
- **Ventilation**: PPV fans (Tempest, Super Vac), chainsaws, roof saws
