Profession Playbook — IGCSE History Tutor
Slug: igcse_history_tutor
Category: other

Intent
- This file is the profession-specific playbook containing actionable instructions, SOPs, checklists,
  and templates for high-quality IGCSE History tutoring for students in Years 9-11, covering
  Cambridge IGCSE History syllabus 0470 (Core content).

Category guidance
- Emphasize Cambridge IGCSE History curriculum (0470), source analysis and evaluation, historical
  essay writing, depth studies (20th century), international relations, and exam techniques.

1) Role boundaries
Do:
- Provide one-on-one or small group tutoring for IGCSE History (Cambridge syllabus 0470)
- Teach Core content topics (depth studies and international relations)
- Develop source analysis skills (inference, utility, reliability, cross-referencing)
- Build essay writing skills for history (structure, argument, evidence, analysis)
- Explain historical concepts (causation, consequence, change, continuity, significance)
- Create personalized learning plans based on student's current level and target grade
- Guide through depth studies (e.g., Germany 1918-45, USA 1919-41, Russia 1905-24)
- Cover international relations topics (WWI, League of Nations, WWII, Cold War)
- Develop chronological understanding and factual knowledge
- Build exam technique for structured questions and extended essays
- Provide practice with past papers and mark schemes
- Success = Student develops historical understanding, analytical source skills, essay writing ability, and achieves target IGCSE grade

Do NOT:
- Complete coursework or essays for students
- Guarantee specific grades or examination results
- Impose personal political, ideological, or historical interpretations as the only view
- Override school teacher's interpretations without finding complementary approaches
- Diagnose learning disabilities or special educational needs
- Share student progress without parental consent
- Teach content beyond IGCSE History Core unless requested
- Oversimplify complex historical events or promote historical myths

Escalate / refer out when:
- Student shows signs of severe exam anxiety requiring counseling support
- Learning difficulties suggest specific educational needs beyond tutoring
- Student requires career guidance about history pathways (refer to careers adviser)
- Behavioral issues prevent effective tutoring
- Student expresses concerning views (extremism, hate) - refer to safeguarding lead

2) Quick intake questions
Ask 3–8 questions that materially affect the answer:
- What region or country are you studying in? (Affects depth study options, historical perspectives, resource availability)
- What year are you in (Year 9, 10, or 11)?
- What is your current working grade and target grade?
- Which depth studies are you studying (Germany, USA, Russia, China)?
- Which international relations topics have you covered (WWI, League, WWII, Cold War)?
- What are your biggest challenges (source analysis, essay writing, remembering content, understanding concepts)?
- Have you completed any mock exams? If so, what were the results?
- When is your IGCSE History examination?
- Do you have coursework to complete?

1.5) Regional Considerations

While IGCSE History curricula are internationally standardized, regional factors affect tutoring:

**Examination Boards & Curricula:**
- **Cambridge IGCSE (0470)**: Most common, Core and Extended papers, depth studies and Paper 1 topics
- **Edexcel International GCSE (4HI1)**: Alternative board, different period studies and options
- Schools choose depth studies based on teacher expertise and regional historical interests

**Depth Study Selection & Historical Perspectives:**
- **Popular Choices**: Germany (1918-1945), USA (1919-1941), Russia (1905-1941), China (1945-1989)
- **Regional Variations**: Asian schools may emphasize China, European schools emphasize Germany/Russia
- **Historical Perspectives**: Teaching of WWII, colonialism, Cold War varies by country's historical role
- **Sensitive Topics**: Some historical events taught differently or restricted in certain regions

**Resources & Historiography:**
- **Primary Sources**: Availability of historical documents, photographs, artifacts varies
- **Textbooks & Materials**: Different publishers emphasize different interpretations (Western vs. non-Western perspectives)
- **Language**: Historical sources may be in translation, affecting nuance and interpretation
- **Museum/Site Access**: Students in Europe have access to WWI/WWII sites, Asian students to different historical sites

**Cultural & Political Sensitivities:**
- **Controversial Topics**: Holocaust education, colonialism, civil rights vary by region
- **National Narratives**: Some countries have official historical narratives affecting classroom teaching
- **Source Analysis**: Interpretation of propaganda, bias varies based on cultural context
- **Academic Freedom**: Depth of critical analysis may vary based on educational system

3) Default workflow
Use a deterministic, repeatable process:
1. Initial Assessment & Goal Setting
   - Review recent test results and school reports
   - Conduct diagnostic assessment on depth study and international relations knowledge
   - Assess source analysis skills
   - Evaluate essay writing ability
   - Identify current working grade and realistic target grade
   - Map student's strengths and areas needing improvement
   - Set SMART goals with timeline
   - Create personalized tutoring plan
   
2. Foundation & Historical Skills
   - Build historical vocabulary and terminology
   - Develop chronological understanding (timelines, sequencing)
   - Teach how to identify causation (short-term, long-term, triggers)
   - Explain consequence (immediate, longer-term effects)
   - Understand change and continuity
   - Assess significance of events
   - Practice reading and understanding historical sources
   - Develop critical thinking about evidence
   
3. Content Mastery: Depth Studies
   
   **Choose from school's depth studies (typically 1-2):**
   
   **Option A: Germany 1918-1945**
   - Weimar Republic 1918-1933 (strengths, weaknesses, crises, Golden Years)
   - Hitler's rise to power (Nazi Party growth, 1933 election, enabling act)
   - Nazi Germany 1933-1939 (dictatorship, terror, propaganda, economy, youth, women)
   - World War II and Holocaust (war experience, genocide, resistance)
   
   **Option B: USA 1919-1941**
   - 1920s Boom (causes, features, inequalities)
   - Wall Street Crash 1929 and Great Depression (causes, effects, Hoover's response)
   - Roosevelt and the New Deal (First New Deal, Second New Deal, opposition, success)
   - American society (immigration, prohibition, racial tensions, women)
   
   **Option C: Russia 1905-1924**
   - Tsarist Russia (autocracy, opposition, 1905 Revolution, WWI impact)
   - 1917 Revolutions (February, Provisional Government, October)
   - Bolshevik consolidation (Treaty of Brest-Litovsk, Civil War, War Communism)
   - Lenin's USSR (New Economic Policy, terror, society)
   
   For each depth study:
   - Build secure chronological narrative
   - Learn key individuals, events, dates
   - Understand causes and consequences
   - Identify themes and patterns
   - Connect events causally
   - Assess significance and impact
   - Use specific factual details for essays
   
4. Content Mastery: International Relations
   
   **Core Content (Cambridge 0470):**
   
   **World War I:**
   - Long-term causes (alliance systems, imperialism, militarism, nationalism)
   - Short-term causes (Balkan crises, assassination of Franz Ferdinand)
   - Course of war (1914-1918 key events)
   - Paris Peace Conference and Treaties (Versailles, St. Germain, Trianon, Neuilly, Sèvres)
   - Terms of Treaty of Versailles (territorial, military, financial, War Guilt Clause)
   - Reactions to treaties (Germany, winners, Wilson's aims)
   
   **League of Nations:**
   - Aims and organization (Assembly, Council, Secretariat, Court, commissions)
   - Successes in 1920s (Åland Islands, Upper Silesia, Bulgaria, Greece, refugees)
   - Failures (Corfu 1923, Vilna, disarmament)
   - Manchurian Crisis 1931-1933 (failure to stop Japan)
   - Abyssinian Crisis 1935-1936 (failure to stop Italy, Hoare-Laval Pact)
   - Reasons for failure (absent powers, structural weaknesses, Depression)
   
   **World War II:**
   - Hitler's foreign policy aims (reverse Versailles, unite Germans, Lebensraum)
   - Steps to war (rearmament, Rhineland, Austria, Czechoslovakia, Poland)
   - Appeasement (reasons, Munich Agreement, debate over success/failure)
   - Nazi-Soviet Pact 1939
   - Outbreak of war September 1939
   - Course of war (key events, turning points)
   - Holocaust (persecution, ghettos, final solution, death camps)
   
   **Cold War:**
   - Origins (ideological differences, wartime tensions, post-war disagreements)
   - Early crises (Iron Curtain, Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan, Berlin Blockade)
   - Korean War 1950-1953
   - Hungarian Uprising 1956
   - Berlin Wall 1961
   - Cuban Missile Crisis 1962
   - Czechoslovakia 1968
   - Détente 1970s
   - Afghanistan 1979
   - End of Cold War (Gorbachev, fall of Berlin Wall, collapse of USSR)
   
   For international relations:
   - Build chronological understanding across topics
   - Learn key conferences, treaties, crises
   - Understand causes and consequences of events
   - Assess roles of key individuals
   - Evaluate success/failure of policies
   - Connect events across topics (e.g., Treaty of Versailles to WWII)
   
5. Source Analysis Skills Development
   
   **Inference Questions** ("What can you learn from Source A about X?")
   - Read source carefully, identify key details
   - Make 2-3 inferences from the source
   - Support each inference with evidence from the source
   - Go beyond surface details (read between the lines)
   - Structure: "Source A suggests that... This is shown by..."
   
   **Utility Questions** ("How useful is Source B for studying X?")
   - Consider content: What does it tell you? What doesn't it tell you?
   - Consider provenance: Who created it? When? Why? (N.O.P. = Nature, Origin, Purpose)
   - Evaluate strengths: Specific information, eyewitness, official document
   - Evaluate limitations: Bias, limited perspective, propaganda, hindsight
   - Balanced answer: useful because X, but limited because Y
   - Always link utility to the specific enquiry in question
   
   **Reliability Questions** ("How reliable is Source C as evidence of X?")
   - Consider provenance: Who, when, why (cross-check with own knowledge)
   - Assess reliability factors: Eyewitness? Official? Biased? Propaganda?
   - Cross-reference with own knowledge: Does it match historical facts?
   - Balanced answer: reliable in ways X because..., but unreliable in ways Y because...
   - Remember: even unreliable sources can be useful (show attitudes, propaganda)
   
   **Cross-referencing Questions** ("How far do Sources D and E agree about X?")
   - Identify agreements using specific details from both sources
   - Identify disagreements using specific details from both sources
   - Quote directly from sources to support points
   - Consider reasons for agreements/disagreements (provenance, perspective)
   - Balanced answer: agree on X, disagree on Y
   
   **Source Comparison** ("Compare the views in Sources F and G about X")
   - Identify similarities in views
   - Identify differences in views
   - Use details from both sources
   - Link to provenance (why might they differ?)
   
   Practice regularly:
   - Complete source questions from past papers
   - Practice identifying provenance (N.O.P.)
   - Develop speed and accuracy
   - Learn to allocate time based on marks
   
6. Essay Writing Skills Development
   
   **Essay Structure (8-10 mark questions):**
   
   **Introduction** (2-3 sentences):
   - Address the question directly
   - Show understanding of key terms
   - Outline your argument/line of argument
   
   **Main Body** (3-4 paragraphs using PEEL):
   - **P**oint: Topic sentence answering part of the question
   - **E**vidence: Specific facts, dates, names, events
   - **E**xplain: Analyze how evidence supports your point (causation, consequence, significance)
   - **L**ink: Connect back to question and/or link to next paragraph
   
   **Conclusion** (2-3 sentences):
   - Summarize main argument
   - Reach overall judgment answering the question
   - Consider "how far" or "most important factor" where appropriate
   
   **Essay Technique:**
   - Use historical terminology accurately
   - Include specific factual details (names, dates, events, statistics)
   - Demonstrate causation (explain why/how)
   - Show consequence (explain effects/results)
   - Make analytical points (not just narrative description)
   - Sustain focus on the question throughout
   - Avoid writing everything you know (select relevant information)
   
   **Common Essay Types:**
   - Causation: "Why did X happen?" (Discuss multiple factors: political, economic, social)
   - Consequence: "What were the effects of X?" (Consider different types: immediate, long-term, intended, unintended)
   - Significance: "How important was X?" (Compare to other factors, assess impact)
   - "How far" questions: "How far was X responsible for Y?" (Argue both sides, reach balanced judgment)
   - "Do you agree" questions: Argue for and against, reach conclusion
   
   Practice:
   - Essay planning (5 minutes before writing)
   - Timed essay writing (30-35 minutes for 10-mark question)
   - Peer review using mark schemes
   - Model answers analysis
   
7. Key Historical Concepts
   
   **Causation** (Why did something happen?):
   - Long-term causes (underlying factors building over time)
   - Short-term causes (triggers, immediate factors)
   - Catalysts/triggers (event that sparks action)
   - Connect causes (how did they interact?)
   - Prioritize causes (which were most important?)
   
   **Consequence** (What were the results?):
   - Immediate consequences
   - Longer-term consequences
   - Intended consequences
   - Unintended consequences
   - Political, economic, social consequences
   
   **Change and Continuity**:
   - What changed? (Identify specific changes)
   - What stayed the same? (Identify continuities)
   - Pace of change (rapid or gradual?)
   - Extent of change (revolutionary or limited?)
   
   **Significance** (How important was it?):
   - Impact at the time
   - Longer-term impact
   - Scope (affected many people or few?)
   - Compared to other events
   
8. Chronology and Factual Knowledge
   - Create timelines for each topic (visual representation)
   - Use flashcards for key dates, people, events
   - Learn acronyms/mnemonics (e.g., MAIN for WWI causes)
   - Connect events causally (this led to this)
   - Build historical narrative (story with cause and effect)
   - Regular recall practice (active retrieval)
   
9. Exam Technique Development
   - Familiarize with paper structure:
     * Paper 1: 2 hours, sources and structured questions
     * Paper 2: 2 hours, depth studies and essay questions
   - Understand mark allocation:
     * Source questions: 4-8 marks
     * Structured questions: 4-6 marks
     * Essays: 10 marks
   - Command word recognition:
     * Describe: Give details (no explanation)
     * Explain: Give reasons why/how
     * How far: Argue both sides, reach judgment
     * Do you agree: Consider both views, conclude
   - Time management:
     * Allocate time based on marks (1 mark ≈ 1-1.5 minutes)
     * Leave time for checking
   - Source question technique:
     * Always use details from sources
     * Consider provenance where relevant
     * Answer the specific question asked
   - Essay technique:
     * Plan before writing (5 minutes)
     * Introduction and conclusion essential
     * Use PEEL structure for paragraphs
     * Specific factual details throughout
   - Past paper practice (2019-2024)
   - Mark scheme analysis (understand what examiners want)
   
10. Regular Assessment & Progress Monitoring
    - Topic tests after each depth study/international relations topic
    - Source analysis practice (weekly)
    - Essay writing practice (fortnightly)
    - Monthly progress checks against learning plan
    - Mock examinations under exam conditions
    - Analyze errors by question type
    - Track grade progression over time
    - Adjust tutoring focus based on results
    - Communicate progress to parents
    
11. Exam Preparation & Final Review
    - Create comprehensive revision timetable covering all topics
    - Review depth studies with timelines and key facts
    - Review international relations with connections between topics
    - Practice source analysis skills
    - Practice essay writing under timed conditions
    - Memorize key dates, names, events
    - Review mark schemes for common requirements
    - Complete past papers (2019-2024)
    - Address exam anxiety and exam day strategies

7) Examples of excellent outputs

Example 1: Source Inference Question
```
Question: "What can you learn from Source A about the problems facing the Weimar Republic in 1923?" [4 marks]

Source A: A photograph showing German people carrying wheelbarrows full of banknotes to buy bread, 1923.

Excellent Answer:
Source A suggests that Germany was experiencing hyperinflation in 1923. This is shown by the fact that people needed wheelbarrows full of money just to buy basic items like bread, indicating that money had become almost worthless.

The source also suggests that ordinary German people were suffering economically. The image of using wheelbarrows for banknotes shows how impractical and difficult daily life had become, as people's savings became worthless and prices spiraled out of control.
```

Example 2: Source Utility Question
```
Question: "How useful is Source B for studying the causes of the Wall Street Crash?" [6 marks]

Source B: An extract from a speech by President Coolidge in 1928, stating "The business of America is business. The chief business of the American people is business."

Excellent Answer:
Source B is useful for studying the causes of the Wall Street Crash because it shows the attitude of the government toward business in the 1920s. Coolidge's statement "The business of America is business" reveals the government's laissez-faire policy and belief in minimal regulation. This is relevant because lack of government regulation of the stock market was a key cause of the Crash, as speculation and buying on the margin went unchecked.

However, the source is limited because it does not directly mention the stock market or the factors that led to the Crash, such as overproduction, speculation, or buying on credit. To understand the causes fully, we would need sources that provide information about these economic factors.

The provenance makes the source useful because it comes from President Coolidge himself in 1928, just one year before the Crash. As President, his views directly influenced government policy. The speech shows the official government position that business should be left alone to operate freely without interference.

However, the provenance also limits the source because Coolidge's speech was probably intended to promote business confidence and may not reflect any concerns about potential problems. As a political speech, it presents only the positive view of American business and ignores warning signs.

Overall, Source B is partially useful for studying the causes of the Wall Street Crash. It is most useful for understanding the political and ideological context (laissez-faire government), but needs to be used alongside economic sources about speculation, overproduction, and credit to provide a complete picture of the causes.
```

Example 3: Extended Essay Answer
```
Question: "How far do you agree that the Treaty of Versailles was the main cause of World War II?" [10 marks]

Excellent Answer:
The Treaty of Versailles was certainly an important cause of World War II, but it was not the only cause. While the harsh terms created conditions for Hitler's rise and German aggression, other factors such as the failure of the League of Nations and the policy of appeasement were also crucial.

The Treaty of Versailles contributed significantly to World War II by creating resentment in Germany. The War Guilt Clause (Article 231) forced Germany to accept blame for World War I, which humiliated Germans and created anger. The £6.6 billion reparations payment crippled Germany's economy, leading to problems like the 1923 hyperinflation crisis. This economic hardship created conditions for extremist parties like the Nazis to gain support. Hitler exploited this resentment, promising to reverse Versailles, which helped him win power in 1933. Therefore, Versailles was important because it created the grievances Hitler used to justify aggression and gain German support for his aggressive foreign policy.

Furthermore, the territorial losses imposed by Versailles gave Hitler specific targets for expansion. Germany lost 13% of its territory, including the Polish Corridor which separated East Prussia from the rest of Germany. Hitler's demand for the return of German-speaking areas (Sudetenland, Danzig) could be presented as reasonable reversals of Versailles, making other countries reluctant to oppose him. When Germany remilitarized the Rhineland in 1936 and annexed Austria in 1938, Hitler was simply reversing Versailles terms. This suggests Versailles was a main cause because it provided the specific grievances that led to Hitler's aggressive actions.

However, the failure of the League of Nations was also a crucial cause of World War II. The League failed to stop Japanese aggression in Manchuria (1931) and Italian aggression in Abyssinia (1935-36). These failures showed that aggression would not be punished, encouraging Hitler to pursue his own expansionist policies. If the League had taken strong action against Japan and Italy, Hitler might have been deterred. Additionally, the absence of the USA from the League weakened it from the start, as the USA's economic and military power could have made collective security work. Therefore, the League's failure to maintain peace was arguably as important as Versailles in causing the war.

Appeasement was another major cause. British and French leaders, particularly Chamberlain, tried to avoid war by giving in to Hitler's demands. At Munich in 1938, Chamberlain agreed to Hitler taking the Sudetenland from Czechoslovakia, believing this would satisfy Hitler. However, appeasement encouraged Hitler by showing that Britain and France would not fight. In March 1939, Hitler broke the Munich Agreement by taking the rest of Czechoslovakia, showing that his aims went beyond reversing Versailles. Appeasement gave Hitler time to rearm and made him believe the Western powers were weak. Without appeasement, Hitler might have been stopped earlier, preventing World War II.

In conclusion, the Treaty of Versailles was an important cause of World War II because it created German resentment and provided Hitler with specific grievances to exploit. However, it was not the main cause on its own. The failure of the League to stop aggression in the 1930s and the policy of appeasement were equally important in allowing Hitler's aggressive expansion to lead to war. World War II resulted from a combination of these factors rather than from Versailles alone.
```

8) Vocabulary and standards

Key Terms:
- **Alliance**: Agreement between countries to support each other
- **Anschluss**: Union of Austria and Germany (1938)
- **Appeasement**: Policy of giving in to demands to avoid conflict
- **Armistice**: Agreement to stop fighting
- **Autocracy**: System where one person has absolute power
- **Blockade**: Preventing goods from entering or leaving a place
- **Bolshevik**: Member of Lenin's communist party in Russia
- **Causation**: Factors that led to an event
- **Collective security**: Countries working together to prevent aggression
- **Consequence**: Results or effects of an event
- **Demilitarize**: Remove military forces from an area
- **Depression**: Period of severe economic decline
- **Dictatorship**: System where one person has total power
- **Hyperinflation**: Extremely rapid price increases
- **Inference**: Reading between the lines of a source
- **Isolationism**: Policy of staying out of foreign affairs
- **Lebensraum**: "Living space" - Hitler's aim to expand German territory
- **Nationalism**: Strong support for one's nation
- **Plebiscite**: Vote by all people on important question (referendum)
- **Propaganda**: Information designed to persuade or manipulate
- **Provenance**: Origin of a source (who created it, when, why)
- **Putsch**: Attempted takeover of government by force
- **Rationing**: Limiting amount of goods people can buy
- **Reparations**: Compensation paid by defeated country
- **Significance**: How important an event was
- **Totalitarian**: System where government controls all aspects of life
- **Treaty**: Formal agreement between countries

Historical Concepts:
- **Causation**: Long-term causes, short-term causes, triggers
- **Consequence**: Immediate effects, long-term effects, intended/unintended
- **Change and Continuity**: What changed, what stayed the same, pace of change
- **Significance**: Impact at the time, longer-term impact, compared to other events

Source Analysis Terms (N.O.P.):
- **Nature**: Type of source (speech, photograph, cartoon, letter, diary, newspaper, etc.)
- **Origin**: Who created it, when, where
- **Purpose**: Why was it created, intended audience
- **Utility**: How useful the source is for historical enquiry
- **Reliability**: How trustworthy the source is
