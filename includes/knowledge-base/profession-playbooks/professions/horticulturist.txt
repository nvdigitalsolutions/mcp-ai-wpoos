Profession Playbook — Horticulturist

Intent
- Profession-specific playbook for horticulture, ornamental plant production, landscape maintenance.
- Contains instructions for plant selection, cultivation, pest management, and landscape design support.

1) Role boundaries
Do:
- Recommend ornamental plants for specific sites and purposes
- Advise on plant care (watering, fertilization, pruning, mulching)
- Diagnose plant problems (pests, diseases, nutrient deficiencies, environmental stress)
- Design and manage integrated pest management programs
- Guide on propagation techniques (seeds, cuttings, grafting, tissue culture)
- Recommend soil amendments and fertility programs for ornamentals
- Advise on greenhouse and nursery production
- Guide on sustainable landscaping (native plants, water conservation, pollinator habitat)
- Recommend post-harvest handling for cut flowers and ornamentals
- Success = Healthy plants, aesthetically pleasing landscapes, sustainable practices, client satisfaction

Do NOT:
- Apply pesticides without proper applicator licensing
- Provide landscape architecture services without licensure
- Make structural engineering recommendations for hardscaping
- Guarantee plant survival without site assessment
- Diagnose human health effects from plant exposures (defer to physicians)
- Make final determinations on invasive species regulations
- Provide tree risk assessments without certified arborist credentials

Escalate when:
- Tree diseases require certified arborist or plant pathologist
- Large tree removals require arborist and safety expertise
- Irrigation system design requires landscape architect or engineer
- Major pest outbreaks require extension specialist or consultant
- Soil contamination requires environmental assessment
- Historic landscapes require preservation specialist

2) Quick intake questions (5-8)
- What is the intended use (residential, commercial, public, production)?
- What are site conditions (sun, soil, drainage, hardiness zone)?
- What are aesthetic preferences (formal, informal, native, exotic)?
- What is maintenance capacity (intensive, moderate, low)?
- Are there constraints (budget, water restrictions, HOA rules)?
- What are existing problems (poor growth, pests, diseases)?
- What are sustainability goals (water conservation, pollinator support)?
- What is the timeline for installation/results?

3) Default workflow
1. Site Assessment
   - Determine hardiness zone and microclimate
   - Assess soil (texture, pH, drainage, fertility)
   - Evaluate sun exposure and shade patterns
   - Identify existing vegetation and problems
   - Note infrastructure (irrigation, lighting, utilities)
   - Consider surrounding landscape context

2. Plant Selection
   - Match plants to site conditions (right plant, right place)
   - Consider mature size and growth rate
   - Select appropriate cultivars for climate
   - Balance aesthetics with function
   - Incorporate diversity for resilience
   - Choose disease-resistant varieties
   - Consider native plants and pollinator value

3. Planting and Establishment
   - Prepare planting site (soil amendment, drainage)
   - Plant at proper depth and spacing
   - Mulch appropriately (2-4" organic mulch)
   - Provide adequate water during establishment
   - Stake trees if needed (remove after 1 year)
   - Protect from browsing or other damage

4. Ongoing Care and Maintenance
   - Irrigate based on plant needs and rainfall
   - Fertilize according to soil tests and plant requirements
   - Prune for health, structure, and aesthetics
   - Manage weeds (mulch, hand-pull, selective herbicides)
   - Monitor for pests and diseases
   - Divide perennials as needed
   - Refresh mulch annually

5. Integrated Pest Management
   - Scout regularly for pests and diseases
   - Identify problems accurately
   - Determine action thresholds
   - Use cultural controls (sanitation, resistant varieties, proper watering)
   - Use biological controls (beneficial insects, microbials)
   - Apply pesticides judiciously following IPM principles and labels

6. Problem Diagnosis and Troubleshooting
   - Assess symptoms (leaves, stems, roots, flowers)
   - Distinguish biotic (pests, diseases) from abiotic (environmental) problems
   - Check for nutrient deficiencies
   - Evaluate cultural practices
   - Send samples to diagnostic lab if needed
   - Develop corrective action plan

4) Deliverables
- Plant Recommendation List with site suitability notes
- Planting Plan with layout and specifications
- Maintenance Schedule and Calendar
- Pest and Disease Management Plan
- Fertilization and Soil Amendment Program
- Irrigation Schedule
- Problem Diagnosis Report with solutions
- Sustainable Landscape Design with native/pollinator plants
- Production Schedule for nursery/greenhouse

Minimum: Basic plant list, simple care instructions
Gold standard: Comprehensive planting plan with site-specific selections, detailed maintenance calendar, IPM program, soil fertility program, water budget, long-term management strategy

Example 1: Residential Landscape Plant Recommendations
"Shade Garden for North-Facing Foundation Planting (Zone 6b, clay soil, dry shade):

Site Assessment:
- Location: North side of house, receives <2 hours direct sun
- Soil: Heavy clay, poor drainage, pH 7.2
- Existing conditions: Dry due to roof overhang and tree root competition
- Planting area: 40 feet × 4 feet bed
- Maintenance level: Low (homeowner prefers minimal care)
- Aesthetic goal: Year-round interest, low height near windows

Soil Preparation Recommendations:
- Amend with 3-4 inches compost to improve structure and moisture retention
- Add gypsum (5 lbs per 100 sq ft) to help break up clay
- Create gentle berm to improve drainage if ponding observed
- Mulch with 2-3 inches shredded bark after planting

Recommended Plant Palette:

1. Structural Foundation Plants (3-4 feet mature height):
   - Soft Touch Holly (Ilex crenata 'Soft Touch'): 3 plants, spaced 4 feet, evergreen, compact mounding habit, adapts to dry shade
   - Inkberry Holly (Ilex glabra 'Strongbox'): 2 plants, spaced 5 feet, native, glossy evergreen foliage, tolerates clay

2. Shade Perennials for Texture & Color:
   - Hosta 'Hal​cyon': 5 plants, blue-green foliage, lavender flowers July, slug-resistant
   - Japanese Forest Grass (Hakonechloa macra 'Aureola'): 4 plants, cascading golden foliage, fall color
   - Lenten Rose (Helleborus × hybridus): 7 plants, early spring flowers (March), evergreen foliage
   - Astilbe 'Deutschland': 5 plants, white plumes June-July, fernlike foliage

3. Groundcovers:
   - Bigroot Geranium (Geranium macrorrhizum): 15 plants, fragrant foliage, pink flowers May, excellent dry shade tolerance
   - Wild Ginger (Asarum canadense): 10 plants, native, heart-shaped leaves, spreads slowly

Planting Plan (front to back from house):
Row 1 (closest to foundation): Soft Touch & Inkberry Holly alternating
Row 2 (18" from Row 1): Helleborus interspersed with Astilbe
Row 3 (18" from Row 2): Hosta and Japanese Forest Grass alternating
Row 4 (front edge): Geranium and Wild Ginger as groundcover

Maintenance Schedule:
- Year 1: Water deeply weekly during establishment (skip if rain >1")
- Spring (April): Cut back Helleborus old foliage before new growth, divide Hosta if overcrowded (every 4-5 years)
- Late Spring (May): Apply slow-release fertilizer (10-10-10) at half rate
- Summer: Deadhead Astilbe after bloom, cut back Japanese Forest Grass if ratty (optional)
- Fall (October): Leave perennial foliage for winter interest, refresh mulch
- Ongoing: Monitor for slugs on Hosta (use iron phosphate bait if severe)

Expected Cost:
- Plants: $350 (purchasing 1-gallon containers)
- Soil amendments: $75
- Mulch: $40
- Total: $465

Expected Outcome:
- Low-maintenance planting requiring minimal water after Year 1
- Year-round structure from evergreens
- Spring interest: Helleborus flowers (March), Astilbe foliage emerges (April)
- Summer: Astilbe blooms (June-July), Hosta flowers (July)
- Fall: Japanese Forest Grass golden color (September-October)
- Winter: Evergreen foliage of Helleborus, hollies, and Geranium provides structure

Alternative Options (if budget constraints):
- Substitute less expensive cultivars (standard Hosta varieties $5 vs. $12)
- Start with smaller plants (4" pots) and allow longer establishment: savings $150, takes 2 years to fill in
- Phase installation: Year 1 hollies + groundcovers ($200), Year 2 perennials ($150)"

Example 2: Integrated Pest Management for Rose Diseases
"Black Spot and Powdery Mildew Management on Hybrid Tea Roses:

Problem: Client has 12 hybrid tea roses showing severe black spot (Diplocarpon rosae) defoliation by mid-summer and powdery mildew (Podosphaera pannosa) on new growth. Plants weakened, few blooms, aesthetically unacceptable.

IPM Assessment:

Cultural Factors Contributing to Disease:
- Dense planting (2 feet apart, should be 3-4 feet for air circulation)
- Overhead irrigation wetting foliage daily (encourages fungal infections)
- Poor air circulation (roses planted against fence)
- No fall sanitation (leaves with black spot left on ground, overwinter inoculum)
- Susceptible cultivars selected ('Mr. Lincoln', 'Chrysler Imperial' highly prone)

IPM Strategy (Integrated Cultural, Biological, Chemical):

1. Cultural Controls (Primary focus):
   - Improve spacing: Remove every other plant, relocate to separate bed with proper spacing
   - Switch to drip irrigation or soaker hoses to keep foliage dry
   - Water early morning so any splash dries quickly
   - Prune to open center of plants for air flow (remove crossing canes)
   - Fall sanitation: Rake and destroy all fallen leaves (do not compost)
   - Spring dormant oil spray to kill overwintering fungal spores
   - Apply 2-3" mulch to prevent soil splash onto lower leaves

2. Resistant Variety Replacement (Long-term):
   - Gradually replace susceptible hybrids with disease-resistant varieties:
     * 'Knock Out' series: Excellent black spot and mildew resistance, continuous bloom
     * 'Bonica': Shrub rose, disease resistant, low maintenance
     * David Austin English roses: 'Lady of Shalott', 'The Generous Gardener' (moderate resistance)
   - Keep 3-4 favorite hybrid teas but manage intensively

3. Biological Controls:
   - Apply Bacillus subtilis (Serenade) or Streptomyces lydicus (Actinovate) biofungicides
   - Alternate with potassium bicarbonate (MilStop) to prevent resistance
   - Apply every 7-10 days during periods of high disease pressure (humid, warm weather)
   - Spray preventively before symptoms appear (impossible to cure established infections)

4. Chemical Controls (Last Resort, Alternated to Prevent Resistance):
   - Systemic fungicides (Immunox, Banner Maxx) containing myclobutanil or propiconazole
   - Contact fungicides (Mancozeb, chlorothalonil) for protectant coverage
   - Rotation schedule: Week 1 - Cultural + Biological, Week 2 - Cultural + Biological, Week 3 - Cultural + Chemical A, Week 4 - Cultural + Biological, Week 5 - Cultural + Chemical B, repeat
   - Spray to point of runoff, cover upper and lower leaf surfaces
   - Follow label instructions precisely (PHI, PPE, rates)

5. Monitoring & Scouting:
   - Weekly inspection of new growth for powdery mildew (white powder on leaves/buds)
   - Check lower/older leaves for black spot (circular black lesions with fringed margins)
   - Note weather conditions: warm humid periods = high infection risk
   - Track disease incidence: % leaves affected, defoliation level
   - Adjust spray schedule based on conditions (more frequent during wet periods)

Implementation Timeline:

Year 1 (Current Season - Mitigation):
- Immediate: Install drip irrigation, improve spacing, prune for air flow
- Weekly through October: Spray rotation (biologicals + limited chemical when severe)
- Fall: Thorough sanitation, dormant oil spray after leaf drop
- Expected outcome: Reduce disease severity 50%, improve plant vigor

Year 2 (Intensive Management):
- Spring: Preventive spray program starting at bud break
- Replace 4 most susceptible/poor-performing roses with resistant varieties
- Continue monitoring and spray rotation
- Expected outcome: Minimal disease, good bloom production

Year 3+ (Sustainable Management):
- Maintain cultural controls (spacing, irrigation, sanitation)
- Primarily biological controls with rare chemical intervention
- Complete transition to resistant varieties (replace remaining susceptible roses)
- Expected outcome: Low-maintenance roses with occasional spot treatment only

Cost-Benefit Analysis:
- Current intensive management: $300/year (fungicides, irrigation supplies, labor)
- After transition to resistant varieties: $50/year (basic care, occasional biocontrol)
- Resistant variety purchase: $25-35/plant × 8 plants = $200-280 (one-time)
- ROI: Break-even in Year 2, long-term savings $250/year

Success Metrics:
- Reduce defoliation from 70% to <20% by end of Year 1
- Maintain >80% foliar retention through growing season Years 2+
- Reduce fungicide applications from 15-20/season to <5/season
- Client satisfaction with appearance and reduced maintenance burden"

Example 3: Greenhouse Production Schedule for Spring Annuals
"10,000 sq ft Commercial Greenhouse - Spring Bedding Plant Production:

Production Goal: Produce 50,000 units (flats and 4" pots) of spring annuals for retail sales April 15 - June 1

Crop Planning:

1. Crop Mix (Based on Market Demand):
   - Petunias: 15,000 units (30%) - High demand, long bloom
   - Impatiens: 10,000 units (20%) - Shade bedding staple
   - Marigolds: 8,000 units (16%) - Easy, reliable
   - Geraniums: 7,000 units (14%) - Higher value, cuttings
   - Tomato transplants: 5,000 units (10%) - Vegetable starts
   - Specialty annuals: 5,000 units (10%) - Salvia, verbena, calibrachoa

2. Sowing Schedule (Working Backward from Sale Date):

Crop         | Weeks to Finish | Seed/Start Date | Sale Date Range
Tomatoes     | 6-7 weeks      | March 1         | April 15-May 15
Petunias     | 10-12 weeks    | February 1      | April 20-June 1
Impatiens    | 12-14 weeks    | January 15      | May 1-June 1
Marigolds    | 7-8 weeks      | March 1         | April 20-May 30
Geraniums    | 12-14 weeks*   | January 1*      | April 15-May 30
Salvia       | 8-10 weeks     | February 15     | April 25-May 30

*Geraniums from rooted cuttings received January 1

3. Growing Conditions by Crop:

Petunias (Petunia × hybrida):
- Germination: 70-75°F, 5-7 days, light required
- Growing: 62-65°F nights, 70-75°F days
- Fertilization: 150-200 ppm N constant liquid feed (20-10-20)
- Pinch once at 4-5 true leaves for branching
- Growth regulators: B-Nine 2500 ppm if stretching
- Common issues: Botrytis if too wet/humid, aphids, whiteflies

Impatiens (Impatiens walleriana):
- Germination: 72-75°F, 7-10 days, needs light
- Growing: 65-68°F nights, consistent moisture critical
- Fertilization: 100-150 ppm N constant feed (20-10-20)
- Shade required (50% shadecloth) to prevent leaf scorch
- Pinch for branching, use growth retardant (B-Nine) sparingly
- Common issues: Impatiens downy mildew (use resistant varieties), pythium root rot if overwatered

Tomatoes (Solanum lycopersicum):
- Germination: 75-80°F, 5-7 days
- Growing: 60-65°F nights, 70-75°F days, high light
- Fertilization: 200-250 ppm N (20-10-20), transition to 15-5-15 at transplant
- Transplant from plug tray to 4" pot at 2-3 true leaves
- Harden off 7-10 days before sale (reduce temperature, water stress)
- Provide plant tags with variety info and care instructions

4. Integrated Pest Management:

Monitoring:
- Weekly scouting with yellow sticky cards (monitor for fungus gnats, whiteflies, aphids)
- Inspect new plant shipments for pests before placing in greenhouse
- Check for disease symptoms during irrigation (botrytis, root rots)

Prevention:
- Sanitation: Clean benches, remove plant debris daily, disinfect pots/flats
- Proper spacing for air circulation (avoid overcrowding)
- Water early in day so foliage dries before evening
- Screen vents to exclude pests
- Quarantine suspect plants immediately

Biological Controls:
- Release beneficial insects preventively:
  * Encarsia formosa (parasitoid wasp) for whitefly control
  * Aphidius colemani (parasitoid wasp) for aphids
  * Hypoaspis miles (predatory mite) for fungus gnats
- Apply Bacillus thuringiensis var. israelensis (Gnatrol) for fungus gnat larvae in growing media

Chemical Controls (Spot Treatment Only):
- Rotate insecticides to prevent resistance (Safari, Conserve, Endeavor)
- Use selective products compatible with biological controls when possible
- Follow label strictly for PHI, application rates, PPE

6. Post-Harvest & Sales:

Quality Control at Sale:
- Plants fully rooted, no root-bound specimens
- Compact, well-branched habit
- Free of pests and diseases
- Color showing (buds or flowers present)
- Proper labeling with variety and care instructions

Retail Display:
- Group by sun requirements (sun, part-shade, shade)
- Maintain consistent watering (automated drip for displays)
- Monitor for stress and cull substandard plants
- Rotate stock (first in, first out)

Unsold Inventory Management:
- Discount end-of-season inventory (June) by 30-50%
- Transplant select specimens to larger containers for later summer sales
- Dispose of poor-quality plants (do not sell substandard material)

Lessons Learned from Season:
- Early petunia sales strongest (start additional crop 2 weeks earlier next year)

- Tomato transplants sold out quickly - increase production 20%
- Geranium zonal varieties outperform seed geraniums - consider all cutting-propagated next year
