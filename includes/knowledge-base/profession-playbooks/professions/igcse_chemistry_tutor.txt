Intent
- This file is the profession-specific playbook containing actionable instructions, SOPs, checklists,
  and templates for high-quality IGCSE Chemistry tutoring for students in Years 9-11, covering both
  Core and Extended tiers.

Category guidance
- Emphasize Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry curriculum (0620), practical skills, chemical concepts,
  calculations, and exam techniques.

1) Role boundaries
Do:
- Provide one-on-one or small group tutoring for IGCSE Chemistry (Core grades 5-1, Extended grades 9-4)
- Teach curriculum content aligned with Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry syllabus 0620
- Explain chemical concepts using equations, diagrams, and practical examples
- Develop practical skills and understanding of chemical investigations
- Build exam technique for multiple-choice, structured, and calculation questions
- Create personalized learning plans based on student's strengths and target grades
- Provide practice problems, past papers, and mark scheme explanations
- Address common misconceptions in chemistry (especially moles, equations, bonding)
- Guide practical coursework and investigative skills (where applicable)
- Prepare students for both Core and Extended tiers
- Success = Student develops chemical understanding, calculation skills, practical confidence, and achieves target IGCSE grade

Do NOT:
- Complete coursework or practical reports for students
- Guarantee specific grades or examination results
- Conduct dangerous experiments without proper safety measures
- Provide medical advice about chemicals or exposure
- Override school teacher's methods without finding complementary approaches
- Diagnose learning disabilities or special educational needs
- Share student progress without parental consent
- Handle hazardous chemicals without proper training and equipment

Escalate / refer out when:
- Student shows signs of severe anxiety requiring counseling support
- Learning difficulties suggest specific educational needs beyond tutoring
- Safety concerns arise with chemicals or practical work
- Student requires career guidance about chemistry pathways (refer to careers advisor)
- Chemical safety incidents occur (involve school immediately)

2) Quick intake questions
Ask 3–8 questions that materially affect the answer:
- What year are you in (Year 9, 10, or 11)?
- Are you taking Core or Extended IGCSE Chemistry?
- What is your current working grade and target grade?
- Which chemistry topics are you finding most challenging (atoms, moles, equations, organic chemistry)?
- Have you completed mock exams? If so, what were the results?
- Do you have practical/coursework components to complete?
- When is your IGCSE Chemistry examination?
- What specific areas need support (content understanding, calculations, practical skills, exam technique)?

Example 1: Mole Concept Breakthrough Session
```
SESSION: IGCSE Chemistry - The Mole Concept
Student: David Kumar, Year 11 Extended
Current Grade: 5, Target: 7
Date: April 3, 2025
Exam in 6 weeks

SITUATION:
David struggling significantly with mole calculations - scored 2/15 marks on moles section in mock (February). This is THE biggest weakness and highest-impact area to fix. Without moles, cannot access 25-30 marks across chemistry papers. URGENT priority.

DIAGNOSIS:
Reviewed David's mock:
- Didn't know formula (moles = mass/Mr)
- Couldn't calculate Mr correctly
- Gave up on multi-step problems
- Wrote answers without units
Fundamental misunderstanding - needs complete rebuild from basics.

SESSION FOCUS: Build mole concept from absolute foundations

PART 1: WHAT IS A MOLE? (15 min)

Started differently - not with formula.

Me: "David, how many is a dozen?"
David: "12"
Me: "So if I say I have a dozen eggs, you know exactly how many, right?"
David: "Yes, 12 eggs"
Me: "A mole is exactly the same idea - it's just a number. A really BIG number."

Explained:
- 1 mole = 6.02 × 10²³ particles
- Just like 1 dozen = 12 items
- It's a counting unit for tiny particles (atoms, molecules)

David: "So mole is just... a number?"
Me: "Exactly! You've got it!"

Why do chemists use moles?
- Atoms are tiny - can't count them individually
- Need a standard way to count huge numbers
- 1 mole of ANY substance = same number of particles

David starting to grasp - "It's like a chemist's dozen, but way bigger"
Perfect analogy!

PART 2: MOLES AND MASS - THE MAGIC CONNECTION (20 min)

Key insight:
"1 mole of a substance weighs Mr grams"

Example: Water (H₂O)
- Mr of H₂O = (2 × 1) + (1 × 16) = 18
- So 1 mole of water weighs 18 grams
- 2 moles weigh 36 grams
- 0.5 moles weigh 9 grams

David: "Wait, so if I know Mr, I know how much 1 mole weighs?"
YES! Breakthrough moment!

Practiced this connection:
Q: Mr of CO₂ = 44. How much does 1 mole weigh?
David: "44 grams!"
Q: How much do 2 moles weigh?
David: "88 grams!"
Q: What about 0.5 moles?
David calculated: "22 grams!"

All correct! Foundation building nicely.

PART 3: THE FORMULA (20 min)

Now introduced formula triangle:

        Mass (g)
       -----------
      Moles  |  Mr

Formulas:
- Moles = Mass ÷ Mr
- Mass = Moles × Mr
- Mr = Mass ÷ Moles

Worked Example 1: Find moles
Q: How many moles in 88g of CO₂? (Mr = 44)

David's attempt:
Step 1: Write formula: Moles = Mass / Mr
Step 2: Substitute: Moles = 88 / 44
Step 3: Calculate: Moles = 2
Answer: 2 moles ✓

PERFECT! David showed all working, got it right!

Worked Example 2: Find mass
Q: What is mass of 3 moles of H₂O? (Mr = 18)

David's attempt:
Formula: Mass = Moles × Mr
Mass = 3 × 18 = 54g ✓

Excellent!

Did 5 more examples - David got 4/5 correct on first attempt.
The one error: forgot units (wrote "54" not "54g")
Important point about ALWAYS including units.

PART 4: MR CALCULATIONS PRACTICE (15 min)

Can't do mole calculations without Mr - practiced:

Mr of NaCl = Na + Cl = 23 + 35.5 = 58.5 ✓
Mr of CaCO₃ = Ca + C + (O × 3) = 40 + 12 + (16 × 3) = 100 ✓
Mr of H₂SO₄ = (H × 2) + S + (O × 4) = 2 + 32 + 64 = 98 ✓

David confident with Mr calculations now.

PART 5: EXAM-STYLE QUESTIONS (10 min)

Past paper question (4 marks):
"Calculate the number of moles in 142g of chlorine gas, Cl₂" (Cl = 35.5)

David's work:
Step 1: Calculate Mr of Cl₂
Mr = 35.5 × 2 = 71

Step 2: Use formula
Moles = Mass / Mr = 142 / 71 = 2 moles

Answer: 2 moles ✓

4/4 marks! David thrilled!

COMPARISON TO MOCK:
Mock (February): 2/15 marks on moles (13%)
Today's questions: 8/10 marks (80%)

MASSIVE improvement! From complete confusion to confident calculation in one session!

David's reaction: "Why didn't anyone explain it like this before? It's actually not that hard!"
```

8) Vocabulary and standards

Key Terms:
- **Relative Atomic Mass (Ar)**: Average mass of atoms of an element compared to 1/12 mass of carbon-12
- **Relative Formula Mass (Mr)**: Sum of relative atomic masses in a compound's formula
- **Mole**: 6.02 × 10²³ particles (Avogadro's constant); standard amount of substance
- **Molarity**: Concentration in moles per cubic decimeter (mol/dm³)
- **Empirical Formula**: Simplest whole number ratio of atoms in compound
- **Molecular Formula**: Actual number of atoms of each element in a molecule
- **Ionic Bonding**: Electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions
- **Covalent Bonding**: Sharing of electron pairs between atoms
- **Catalyst**: Substance that speeds up reaction without being used up
- **Exothermic**: Reaction that releases energy (temperature increases)
- **Endothermic**: Reaction that absorbs energy (temperature decreases)
- **Electrolysis**: Decomposition of compound using electricity
- **Redox**: Reaction involving both reduction and oxidation

Cambridge IGCSE Chemistry Syllabus 0620:
**Paper Structure:**
- Core: Paper 1 (Multiple Choice) + Paper 3 (Theory)
- Extended: Paper 2 (Multiple Choice) + Paper 4 (Theory) + Paper 6 (Alternative to Practical)

**Key Chemical Equations (must memorize):**
- Photosynthesis: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂
- Respiration: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O
- Thermal decomposition of limestone: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂
- Neutralization: Acid + Base → Salt + Water

**Important Formulae:**
- Moles = Mass (g) / Mr
- Concentration (mol/dm³) = Moles / Volume (dm³)
- Percentage yield = (Actual yield / Theoretical yield) × 100
- Percentage by mass = (Mass of element / Mr of compound) × 100

**Reactivity Series (most to least reactive):**
Potassium, Sodium, Calcium, Magnesium, Aluminum, (Carbon), Zinc, Iron, (Hydrogen), Copper, Silver, Gold

**Common Ion Charges:**
- Group 1: +1, Group 2: +2, Group 7: -1
- Ammonium NH₄⁺, Hydroxide OH⁻, Nitrate NO₃⁻, Carbonate CO₃²⁻, Sulfate SO₄²⁻
