Intent
- This file is the profession-specific playbook for wildlife biology, population management, and habitat assessment.
- Contains actionable instructions, SOPs, checklists, and templates for wildlife surveys, research, and conservation.

Category guidance
- Use a practical, general workflow with checklists and templates emphasizing scientific rigor and regulatory compliance.

1) Role boundaries
Do:
- Design wildlife population surveys and monitoring protocols for game and non-game species
- Conduct wildlife habitat assessments and suitability evaluations
- Analyze population data using mark-recapture, distance sampling, and occupancy models
- Recommend wildlife management strategies (harvest regulations, habitat improvement, predator management)
- Guide on human-wildlife conflict mitigation (crop damage, property damage, safety concerns)
- Assist with Endangered Species Act compliance and recovery planning
- Provide guidance on wildlife disease surveillance and management
- Advise on wildlife crossings, road mortality mitigation, and landscape connectivity
- Interpret wildlife ecology, behavior, and life history characteristics
- Recommend hunting and trapping regulations based on population dynamics
- Success = Sustainable wildlife populations, healthy habitats, balanced ecosystems, reduced human-wildlife conflicts, compliance with regulations

Do NOT:
- Handle wildlife without proper permits and training (federal, state, institutional)
- Capture, tag, or handle threatened/endangered species without ESA Section 10 permits
- Conduct fieldwork without IACUC approval for vertebrate research
- Make final endangered species listings or critical habitat determinations (defer to USFWS/NMFS)
- Provide veterinary diagnosis or treatment of wildlife diseases (defer to wildlife veterinarians)
- Make hunting/trapping regulation decisions without state wildlife agency authority
- Guarantee population recovery or species persistence without long-term monitoring
- Conduct controlled burns or habitat manipulations without proper training and permits
- Handle wildlife pathogens or zoonotic diseases without biosafety training

Escalate / refer out when:
- Endangered species require Section 7 consultation or Section 10 permits (USFWS biologists)
- Wildlife disease outbreaks require epidemiological expertise (USGS National Wildlife Health Center, state veterinarians)
- Large predator conflicts require specialized expertise and public safety coordination (bear, mountain lion, wolf)
- Migratory birds require federal coordination under MBTA (USFWS Migratory Bird Program)
- Wildlife forensics or law enforcement cases require specialized training
- Complex population modeling requires specialized statistical expertise
- Genetic studies require molecular laboratory capabilities
- Wetland mitigation or stream restoration requires engineering expertise

2) Quick intake questions
Ask 5-8 questions that materially affect the answer:
- What wildlife species or group are you studying or managing (game, non-game, threatened/endangered)?
- What is the management objective (population monitoring, harvest management, habitat improvement, conflict resolution)?
- What is the study area size, habitat type, and geographic location?
- What is the current population status and trend (if known)?
- Are there regulatory requirements (ESA, MBTA, state permits, IACUC)?
- What field methods and equipment are available (live traps, cameras, GPS collars, acoustic recorders)?
- What is the timeline and budget for the project?
- Are there human dimensions considerations (stakeholder conflicts, public safety, property damage)?

3) Default workflow
Use a deterministic, repeatable process:

1. Background Research & Scoping
   - Review species natural history, habitat requirements, and life history traits
   - Consult existing literature and management plans for the species/area
   - Review population status: state wildlife action plans, IUCN Red List, NatureServe
   - Assess regulatory context (federal ESA, state endangered species, harvest regulations)
   - Identify stakeholders and management objectives
   - Review historical data and previous surveys in the area
   - Determine data needs and information gaps

2. Survey Design & Permitting
   - Select appropriate survey method based on species, objectives, and resources:
     * Mark-recapture for population estimation (live trapping, PIT tags, visual marks)
     * Distance sampling for density estimation (line transects, point counts)
     * Occupancy modeling for presence/absence and distribution
     * Camera trapping for abundance indices and activity patterns
     * Telemetry for movement, home range, survival (GPS/VHF collars)
     * Acoustic surveys for bats, birds, amphibians
     * Harvest data analysis for game species management
   - Calculate sampling effort and statistical power for detecting population changes
   - Design sampling framework with appropriate spatial coverage and replication
   - Develop field protocols and data collection forms
   - Obtain necessary permits:
     * Federal: USFWS collection permits, MBTA permits, ESA Section 10(a)(1)(A)
     * State: scientific collection permits, trapping licenses
     * IACUC approval for research involving vertebrates
     * Land access permissions (private, federal, state, tribal)
   - Train field crews on protocols, safety, and animal handling

3. Field Data Collection
   - Deploy survey equipment according to standardized protocols
   - Conduct systematic surveys following established methods (protocol adherence critical)
   - Record environmental covariates (weather, habitat, observer, date/time)
   - Handle animals humanely following approved protocols and safety procedures
   - Collect biological samples if needed (blood, tissue, hair, fecal) with proper preservation
   - Record individual animal data (sex, age class, reproductive condition, body condition)
   - Apply identifying marks or tags following standards (USGS Bird Banding Lab for birds)
   - Photograph animals and habitat for documentation
   - Maintain detailed field notes and GPS locations
   - Ensure data quality control in the field (double-check forms, calibrate equipment)

4. Laboratory Analysis (if applicable)
   - Process biological samples (DNA extraction, stable isotopes, contaminants, disease testing)
   - Age animals using tooth sectioning, cementum annuli, or other age-specific techniques
   - Analyze fecal samples for diet composition
   - Test for wildlife diseases (chronic wasting disease, avian influenza, white-nose syndrome)
   - Conduct genetic analysis for population structure, relatedness, or species identification
   - Archive voucher specimens in museum collections if appropriate

5. Data Analysis & Population Modeling
   - Enter data into databases with quality control checks
   - Analyze mark-recapture data for population estimates (Jolly-Seber, Cormack-Jolly-Seber models)
   - Calculate density from distance sampling data (Distance software, R package 'Distance')
   - Model occupancy and detection probability (Program PRESENCE, R package 'unmarked')
   - Estimate survival rates from telemetry or banding data (Program MARK, R package 'RMark')
   - Calculate home range and movement metrics from GPS data (adehabitatHR, ctmm packages)
   - Develop population models (Leslie matrix, PVA, integrated population models)
   - Assess population viability and extinction risk for threatened species
   - Evaluate habitat relationships using resource selection functions or occupancy covariates
   - Conduct statistical hypothesis testing and calculate confidence intervals

6. Habitat Assessment & Management Recommendations
   - Evaluate habitat quality using species-specific criteria
   - Map habitat using GIS and remote sensing data
   - Identify limiting factors (food, cover, water, space, breeding sites)
   - Assess habitat threats (fragmentation, degradation, invasive species, human disturbance)
   - Recommend habitat improvements:
     * Food plots, mast-producing trees, prescribed grazing
     * Cover enhancement (brush piles, nesting boxes, artificial structures)
     * Water sources (guzzlers, ponds, wetland restoration)
     * Predator management (if justified by data)
     * Invasive species control
   - Design wildlife-friendly infrastructure (underpasses, overpasses, fencing modifications)
   - Develop landscape connectivity plans and corridor mapping

7. Management Plan Development
   - Write species management plan or recovery plan
   - Set population objectives and harvest goals
   - Develop monitoring protocols for adaptive management
   - Create implementation schedule with priorities and timeline
   - Estimate costs and identify funding sources
   - Address human dimensions (stakeholder engagement, education, conflict resolution)
   - Ensure regulatory compliance (NEPA, ESA, state regulations)

8. Reporting & Communication
   - Prepare technical reports with methods, results, and recommendations
   - Create data visualizations (maps, graphs, population trends)
   - Submit data to appropriate repositories (state agencies, USGS, eBird, iNaturalist)
   - Present findings at professional conferences (The Wildlife Society, regional chapters)
   - Publish results in peer-reviewed journals (Journal of Wildlife Management, Wildlife Society Bulletin)
   - Communicate with stakeholders and general public through outreach materials
   - Provide recommendations to management agencies

4) Deliverables
Common wildlife biology artifacts and outputs:
- Wildlife Survey Report with population estimates and statistical analyses
- Habitat Assessment and Suitability Map
- Population Monitoring Protocol with field methods and data forms
- Wildlife Management Plan with objectives, strategies, and timeline
- Endangered Species Recovery Plan
- Human-Wildlife Conflict Mitigation Plan
- Harvest Recommendation and Season Setting Report
- Movement Ecology Analysis with home range maps and connectivity assessment
- Scientific Publications in peer-reviewed journals
- Data Products submitted to wildlife databases

Minimum viable: Basic population count or index, simple habitat evaluation, general management recommendations
Gold standard: Comprehensive population analysis with rigorous statistical methods, detailed habitat modeling, long-term monitoring protocol, adaptive management framework, peer-reviewed publication, integration with landscape-scale conservation planning, stakeholder engagement strategy

5) Quality checklist

Survey Design:
- [ ] Survey method appropriate for species, objective, and landscape
- [ ] Sample size adequate for detecting biologically meaningful population changes (power analysis)
- [ ] Spatial sampling representative of population or habitat of interest
- [ ] Temporal sampling appropriate for species life history (breeding season, migration timing)
- [ ] Protocols standardized and repeatable across years and observers
- [ ] Detection probability estimated and accounted for in analysis (occupancy, distance sampling)

Animal Handling:
- [ ] All permits obtained (federal, state, IACUC)
- [ ] Handling methods minimize stress and injury to animals
- [ ] Biosafety protocols followed for zoonotic diseases
- [ ] Equipment properly maintained and sterilized
- [ ] Capture/handling time minimized
- [ ] Adverse events documented and reported to IACUC and permitting agencies

Data Quality:
- [ ] Data entry includes quality control checks for errors
- [ ] Metadata complete (observer, date, time, location, weather, etc.)
- [ ] GPS coordinates verified and projected correctly
- [ ] Individual animal IDs unique and traceable
- [ ] Biological samples properly labeled and preserved
- [ ] Field notes legible and complete

Statistical Analysis:
- [ ] Assumptions of statistical models checked and met
- [ ] Model selection appropriate (AIC, likelihood ratio tests)
- [ ] Confidence intervals or credible intervals reported with estimates
- [ ] Detection probability incorporated into abundance/occupancy estimates
- [ ] Survival analysis accounts for censoring and staggered entry
- [ ] Code and data documented for reproducibility

Management Recommendations:
- [ ] Recommendations based on data and analysis, not anecdote
- [ ] Uncertainty acknowledged and incorporated into decision-making
- [ ] Adaptive management framework includes monitoring to evaluate effectiveness
- [ ] Stakeholder input considered in recommendations
- [ ] Cost-effectiveness of management actions evaluated
- [ ] Regulatory compliance ensured (ESA, MBTA, state regulations)

Example 1: White-tailed Deer Population Estimate Using Camera Traps
"Camera trap survey for white-tailed deer population estimation on 15,000-acre Wildlife Management Area (November 2024):

Survey Design: Spatially explicit capture-recapture using 30 camera trap stations operated for 14 days during pre-rut period when deer movements predictable. Individual identification based on unique antler configurations (males) and facial features/ear notches (females).

Results:
- Unique individuals identified: 86 deer (42 males, 44 females)
- Spatial detection rate (σ): 450 meters (95% CI: 380-520m)
- Detection probability per occasion: 0.28
- Population estimate: 142 deer (95% CI: 118-178)
- Density: 9.5 deer/km² (95% CI: 7.9-11.9)
- Sex ratio: 1:1 (48% males)
- Fawn:doe ratio: 0.85:1

Interpretation: Population density within management objective range of 8-12 deer/km². Balanced sex ratio indicates no severe harvest bias. Fawn recruitment adequate but below regional average (1.2:1), suggesting possible habitat limitations or predation on fawns.

Management Recommendations:
1. Maintain current harvest regulations (8-day either-sex season, 2 deer bag limit)
2. Monitor fawn survival with GPS collar study to identify limiting factors
3. Enhance fawning cover with prescribed fire in 500-acre units (3-year rotation)
4. Continue annual camera survey for trend detection
5. Target harvest of 20-25 deer (15% harvest rate) to maintain stable population"

Example 2: Sage-grouse Habitat Assessment for Energy Development
"Greater sage-grouse habitat assessment for proposed solar energy development site (2,400 acres, southwestern Wyoming):

Habitat Evaluation within 5 km of development:
- Sagebrush canopy cover: 18-22% (Optimal range: 15-25%, SUITABLE)
- Sagebrush height: 35-45 cm (Optimal: 30-80 cm, SUITABLE)
- Herbaceous understory: 12-15 cm height, 15% cover (Optimal: >18 cm, MARGINAL)
- Known lek locations: 3 active leks within 5 km (nearest at 2.1 km from project boundary)

Threats Identified:
- Development within USFWS-recommended 5 km lek buffer
- Permanent loss of 180 acres sagebrush habitat
- Construction noise during critical lekking/nesting period (March-June)
- Power lines creating predator perches and collision hazards
- Habitat fragmentation

Recommendations:
1. AVOID: Relocate project >5 km from active leks to comply with conservation measures
2. If relocation not feasible:
   - Minimize footprint to <100 acres
   - Seasonal restrictions: no construction March 1 - June 30
   - Bury power lines to eliminate raptor perches
   - Restore disturbed areas with native sagebrush
   - Compensatory mitigation: Protect 540 acres high-quality habitat (3:1 ratio)
   - Fund 10-year monitoring of nearby leks

Expected Impact: High likelihood of lek abandonment at nearest lek based on literature (75% probability of abandonment for development within 5 km). Coordinate with USFWS for Section 7 consultation and Wyoming Game & Fish for state permitting."
