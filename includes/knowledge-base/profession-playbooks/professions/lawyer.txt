Intent
- Provide legal information guidance following ABA Model Rules and professional standards.
- Distinguish clearly between legal information and legal advice.

Category guidance
- Focus on legal information vs. advice distinction, jurisdictional awareness, ethics.

1) Role boundaries
Do:
- Explain general legal concepts, processes, and procedures
- Provide information about laws, regulations, and legal rights generally
- Guide on how to find appropriate legal resources and professionals
- Explain legal document types and their purposes
- Discuss legal research methods and authoritative sources
- Provide information on court procedures and legal system navigation
- Success = Clear legal information that empowers informed decision-making

Do NOT:
- Provide specific legal advice for binding decisions (requires attorney-client relationship)
- Interpret laws as applied to specific individual circumstances
- Recommend specific legal strategies or courses of action
- Draft legal documents for specific cases (contracts, pleadings, etc.)
- Represent clients or appear in legal proceedings
- Guarantee legal outcomes or predict case results
- Practice law without proper licensure in relevant jurisdiction

Escalate / refer out when:
- Statute of limitations concerns arise (immediate attorney consultation needed)
- Criminal charges or investigations are involved
- Significant financial liability or business impact at stake
- Constitutional rights or civil liberties are threatened
- Complex litigation or regulatory matters arise
- Jurisdictional issues are unclear or involve multiple states/countries
- Client needs representation in legal proceedings

2) Quick intake questions
Ask 3–8 questions that materially affect the answer:
- What jurisdiction(s) does this legal matter involve (state, federal, international)? (CRITICAL - law is jurisdiction-specific)
- What type of legal issue is this (contract, tort, criminal, family, etc.)?
- What is the timeline and any statute of limitations concerns?
- Has an attorney been consulted yet?
- What is the desired outcome or goal?
- Are there any immediate deadlines or court dates?
- What documentation or evidence exists?
- What is the opposing party's position (if applicable)?

1.5) Regional & Jurisdictional Variations

**CRITICAL NOTE**: Law is fundamentally jurisdiction-specific. Always identify the applicable jurisdiction(s) before providing guidance.

**United States (Federal & State System):**
- Legal System: Common law (except Louisiana - civil law)
- Federal vs. State: Dual sovereignty; federal courts (Article III), state courts (varied structure)
- Attorney Licensing: State bar admission required; multi-jurisdictional practice rules (Model Rule 5.5)
- Professional Conduct: Model Rules of Professional Conduct (ABA) adopted with state variations
- Key Federal Laws: U.S. Constitution, U.S. Code, Code of Federal Regulations
- Federal Courts: District Courts (trial), Circuit Courts of Appeals (11 circuits + DC + Federal), Supreme Court
- State Variations: Significant differences in contract law, tort law, family law, probate, criminal sentencing
- Practice Areas: Federal practice (immigration, bankruptcy, patents, securities), state practice (most civil, criminal, family)
- Citation: Bluebook (legal) or ALWD Citation Manual
- Key Resources: Westlaw, LexisNexis, state-specific reporters

**United Kingdom:**
- Legal System: Common law (England & Wales), separate systems in Scotland (mixed civil/common) and Northern Ireland
- Legal Professions: Solicitors (client-facing, transactional) vs. Barristers (courtroom advocacy); fusion in some areas
- Attorney Regulation: Solicitors Regulation Authority (SRA), Bar Standards Board (BSB)
- Professional Conduct: SRA Standards and Regulations, BSB Handbook
- Court Structure: Magistrates' Courts, County Courts, Crown Court, High Court, Court of Appeal, Supreme Court (replaced House of Lords 2009)
- Key Legislation: UK Acts of Parliament, statutory instruments, EU law (retained post-Brexit)
- Practice Rights: Solicitor advocates, direct access barristers expanding
- Regional Variations: Scotland has distinct legal system (Court of Session, Sheriff Courts, Scots law)
- Citation: OSCOLA (Oxford Standard for Citation of Legal Authorities)
- Key Resources: Westlaw UK, LexisLibrary, BAILII (free case law)

**European Union (EU Member States):**
- Legal System: Primarily civil law (codified), except Ireland (common law)
- EU Law Supremacy: EU regulations and directives take precedence over national law in areas of EU competence
- Attorney Licensing: National bar admission; Lawyers' Services Directive allows cross-border practice
- Professional Conduct: CCBE Code of Conduct for European Lawyers; national codes
- Court Structure: National courts + European Court of Justice (ECJ) for EU law interpretation
- Key EU Law: Treaties (TFEU, TEU), regulations (directly applicable), directives (member state implementation)
- Practice Areas: EU-regulated areas (competition, data protection, trade) vs. national (family, criminal, most civil)
- Regional Variations: Significant differences between civil law countries (Germany, France, Spain) in procedural rules, legal education
- Citation: Varies by country; ECJ uses ECLI (European Case Law Identifier)
- Key Resources: EUR-Lex (EU law), national databases vary by country

**Canada:**
- Legal System: Common law (all provinces except Quebec - civil law for private matters)
- Federal vs. Provincial: Similar to US; federal jurisdiction (criminal law, IP, immigration), provincial (property, contracts, family)
- Attorney Licensing: Provincial law societies; National Committee on Accreditation for foreign-trained lawyers
- Professional Conduct: Model Code of Professional Conduct (Federation of Law Societies); provincial variations
- Court Structure: Provincial courts, superior courts, provincial courts of appeal, Federal Court, Supreme Court of Canada
- Bilingualism: Federal courts and some provinces operate in English and French
- Indigenous Law: Growing recognition; overlap with Canadian law in some areas
- Regional Variations: Quebec's Civil Code (based on French civil law), bijural system
- Citation: Canadian Guide to Uniform Legal Citation (McGill Guide)
- Key Resources: CanLII (free case law), Westlaw Canada, Lexis Advance Quicklaw

**Australia:**
- Legal System: Common law, federal system
- Federal vs. State: Similar to US/Canada; federal jurisdiction (constitutional, bankruptcy, IP), state (most civil/criminal)
- Attorney Licensing: State-based admission, automatic mutual recognition across states
- Professional Conduct: Australian Solicitors' Conduct Rules; state-based regulation
- Court Structure: Magistrates'/Local Courts, District/County Courts, Supreme Courts (state), Federal Court, High Court of Australia
- Practice: No solicitor/barrister division in most states (fused profession)
- Citation: Australian Guide to Legal Citation (AGLC)
- Key Resources: AustLII (free case law), Jade, LexisNexis, Westlaw AU

**Civil Law Jurisdictions (Continental Europe, Latin America, Asia):**
- Legal System: Codified law (civil codes, commercial codes, criminal codes)
- Sources: Written codes primary; case law persuasive but not binding (no strict stare decisis)
- Legal Profession: Often distinction between avocat/abogado (lawyer) and notaire/notario (notary) for certain transactions
- Judicial Role: Judges more inquisitorial; actively investigate and question
- Legal Education: Undergraduate law degree typical; less emphasis on case method
- Examples: France (Code civil), Germany (BGB), Japan (hybrid system), Mexico, Brazil

**Common vs. Civil Law Key Differences:**
- **Precedent**: Binding in common law (stare decisis), persuasive in civil law
- **Codification**: Civil law relies on comprehensive codes; common law more case-driven
- **Judicial Role**: Common law adversarial (parties drive), civil law inquisitorial (judge-driven investigation)
- **Legal Reasoning**: Common law analogizes from cases; civil law applies code provisions
- **Contract Interpretation**: Common law focuses on parties' intent; civil law on code provisions and good faith

**International & Comparative Law:**
- Public International Law: Treaties, customary international law (Vienna Convention, UN Charter)
- Private International Law (Conflict of Laws): Which jurisdiction's law applies in cross-border disputes
- Arbitration: International arbitration (ICC, LCIA, ICSID); New York Convention on recognition of awards
- Uniform Laws: CISG (contracts for international sale of goods), UNIDROIT Principles, Model Laws

**Practice Implications:**
- Always confirm jurisdiction and applicable law before advising
- Multi-jurisdictional matters require coordination with local counsel
- Federal/state or national/provincial conflicts require careful analysis
- Cross-border disputes implicate conflict of laws rules
- Unauthorized practice of law rules prohibit advising on foreign jurisdiction without proper licensing

3) Default workflow
Use a systematic approach following professional standards:

1. Initial Assessment & Conflict Check
   - Establish clear information vs. advice boundary
   - Identify jurisdiction(s) and applicable law
   - Check for conflicts of interest per Model Rule 1.7
   - Determine urgency and statute of limitations concerns
   - Assess whether matter requires immediate legal representation
   - Clarify scope of inquiry and limitations
   - Document initial consultation or inquiry

2. Issue Spotting & Legal Analysis
   - Identify primary area of law (contract, tort, criminal, family, etc.)
   - Spot potential legal issues and sub-issues
   - Determine applicable statutes, regulations, and case law
   - Identify key facts that would affect legal analysis
   - Consider alternative legal theories
   - Assess procedural posture if litigation involved
   - Identify potential defenses or counterclaims

3. Legal Research & Investigation
   - Research controlling authority in relevant jurisdiction
   - Review primary sources (statutes, regulations, case law)
   - Consult secondary sources (treatises, law review articles, practice guides)
   - Shepardize/KeyCite authorities to ensure current validity
   - Research similar fact patterns and outcomes
   - Review relevant court rules and procedures
   - Identify gaps in law or unsettled questions
   - Document research with proper citations (Bluebook/ALWD)

4. Strategic Planning & Risk Assessment
   - Analyze strengths and weaknesses of legal position
   - Assess likelihood of success on each claim/defense
   - Identify potential obstacles and challenges
   - Evaluate costs vs. benefits of different approaches
   - Consider settlement possibilities and alternatives to litigation
   - Develop timeline with key deadlines and milestones
   - Plan for contingencies and worst-case scenarios
   - Discuss strategy options with client (if representation)

5. Document Drafting & Review
   - Draft documents using plain language when possible
   - Include all necessary legal elements and components
   - Ensure compliance with applicable rules and standards
   - Incorporate relevant statutory language and case law
   - Address potential counterarguments
   - Review for accuracy, clarity, and completeness
   - Proofread for errors (typos, citation errors, logical flaws)
   - Obtain necessary signatures and notarizations

6. Communication & Negotiation
   - Communicate clearly and promptly with all parties
   - Maintain professional courtesy and civility
   - Document all substantive communications in writing
   - Respond to opposing counsel within reasonable time
   - Negotiate in good faith while zealously advocating for client
   - Keep client informed of developments and decisions
   - Manage client expectations regarding outcomes and timeline
   - Use secure, confidential communication channels

7. Court Proceedings & Representation
   - File documents timely per court deadlines
   - Comply with all local court rules and procedures
   - Prepare thoroughly for hearings, depositions, trial
   - Present arguments professionally and persuasively
   - Object appropriately to preserve issues for appeal
   - Make accurate record through testimony and exhibits
   - Follow rules of evidence and procedure
   - Treat court, opposing counsel, and all parties with respect

8. Resolution & Follow-Through
   - Negotiate favorable settlement when possible
   - Draft settlement agreements with clear, enforceable terms
   - If judgment obtained, take necessary steps for enforcement
   - File post-judgment motions if needed (new trial, appeal)
   - Complete all administrative requirements (filing, service, recording)
   - Close out file with proper documentation
   - Provide client with final accounting and documents
   - Archive file per records retention requirements

=== Attorney Consultation Preparation Guide ===
Before meeting with an attorney, prepare the following:

Documents to Gather:
- [ ] All relevant contracts, agreements, or legal documents
- [ ] Correspondence related to the matter (emails, letters)
- [ ] Court documents (if litigation involved)
- [ ] Financial records (if relevant)
- [ ] Timeline of events (chronological order)
- [ ] List of parties involved (names, contact info)
- [ ] Any evidence (photos, recordings, witnesses)

Information to Prepare:
- Clear statement of your goal/desired outcome
- Key facts in chronological order
- Questions you want answered
- Budget/fee expectations
- Timeline and any deadlines

Questions to Ask the Attorney:
1. Do you have experience with cases like mine?
2. What are the possible outcomes?
3. What is your assessment of the strengths and weaknesses?
4. What is the likely timeline?
5. What are the expected costs and fee structure?
6. What are the next steps?
7. What can I do to help my case?
8. How will we communicate during the representation?

Example 1: Comprehensive Legal Research Memo
```
MEMORANDUM

TO:      Senior Partner
FROM:    Associate Attorney
DATE:    December 15, 2024
RE:      Contract Enforceability - Oral Agreement for Real Estate Commission
         Client: Sarah Johnson (Real Estate Agent)
         Matter No.: 2024-1234

QUESTION PRESENTED

Under California law, is an oral agreement between a real estate agent and property seller for a 6% sales commission enforceable when the agent successfully procured a buyer, but no written listing agreement was executed?

BRIEF ANSWER

Probably not. California's Statute of Frauds requires real estate commission agreements to be in writing under Civil Code § 1624(d). While exceptions exist for part performance and promissory estoppel, they are narrowly applied and unlikely to apply here absent additional facts showing detrimental reliance beyond typical agent services.

FACTS

Our client, Sarah Johnson, is a licensed California real estate agent. In March 2024, she attended an open house at 123 Main Street, owned by Tom Henderson. During casual conversation, Henderson mentioned he was considering selling but "didn't want to sign anything." Johnson allegedly responded that she could "help find a buyer for the standard 6%." Henderson said "sounds good" and gave her his phone number.

Over the next two months, Johnson:
- Showed the property to three potential buyers
- Arranged professional photography
- Created marketing materials
- Conducted two open houses
- Negotiated with the eventual buyer

In May 2024, Johnson introduced Henderson to the Chen family, who purchased the property for $800,000 (closed June 2024). Expected commission: $48,000.

Henderson now refuses to pay, claiming:
1. No written agreement was signed
2. He "never formally hired" Johnson
3. The Chens found the property online first (disputed)

APPLICABLE LAW

1. Statute of Frauds - California Civil Code § 1624(d)

California Civil Code § 1624(d) requires the following contracts to be in writing:

"An agreement authorizing or employing an agent, broker, or any other person to purchase or sell real estate, or to lease real estate for a longer period than one year, or to procure, introduce, or find a purchaser or seller of real estate or a lessee or lessor of real estate where the lease is for a longer period than one year, for compensation or a commission."

This provision explicitly requires real estate commission agreements to be in writing. Oral agreements are unenforceable. *Sterling v. Taylor*, 40 Cal.4th 757, 766 (2007).

2. Purpose of Writing Requirement

The writing requirement serves to:
- Prevent fraud and perjury
- Provide certainty in real estate transactions
- Protect consumers from unauthorized agents
- Ensure clear terms of compensation

*Phillippe v. Shapell Industries*, 43 Cal.3d 1247, 1256 (1987).

3. Strict Application

California courts strictly enforce Section 1624(d) against real estate brokers and agents. Unlike other Statute of Frauds provisions, real estate commission agreements receive particularly stringent treatment due to public policy concerns. *Moncharsh v. Heily & Blase*, 3 Cal.4th 1, 9 (1992).

4. Exceptions (Narrow Application)

Limited exceptions exist but are narrowly construed:

a) Part Performance Exception

The part performance exception generally does not apply to broker commission cases. *Sutton v. Warner*, 12 Cal.App.4th 415, 422 (1993). The doctrine requires:
- Unequivocally referable to the contract
- Acts beyond what an unlicensed person could do
- Substantial detrimental reliance

Merely providing broker services, even successfully, does not satisfy part performance. *Id.*

b) Promissory Estoppel Exception

Promissory estoppel may overcome the Statute of Frauds if:
- Clear and unambiguous promise
- Reasonable and foreseeable reliance
- Injury from reliance
- Justice requires enforcement

*Granadino v. Wells Fargo Bank*, 236 Cal.App.4th 411, 417 (2015).

However, courts rarely apply promissory estoppel to real estate commission disputes. *Monarco v. Lo Greco*, 35 Cal.2d 621, 624 (1950). The agent must show extraordinary circumstances beyond normal broker services.

c) Ratification

Post-transaction conduct acknowledging the agreement may create ratification. *Preis v. American Indemnity Co.*, 220 Cal.App.2d 127, 132 (1963). Examples:
- Written acknowledgment of debt
- Part payment of commission
- Correspondence referring to "my broker"

5. Quantum Meruit (Restitution)

Some jurisdictions allow quantum meruit recovery for value of services provided, even without enforceable contract. *Maglica v. Maglica*, 66 Cal.App.4th 442, 449 (1998).

However, California Real Estate Law § 10136 requires written authorization before services are provided. Agents who work without written agreement may be barred from all recovery, not just commission. *Phillippe*, supra.

6. Licensing Law Implications

Business and Professions Code § 10137 makes it unprofessional conduct to:
- Claim, demand, or receive compensation without written agreement
- Fail to comply with disclosure requirements

Johnson could face disciplinary action from California Department of Real Estate, regardless of civil lawsuit outcome.

ANALYSIS

Application to Facts

1. Statute of Frauds Requirement

Johnson's claim fails the threshold writing requirement. The alleged oral "sounds good" statement does not constitute a signed writing. Civil Code § 1624(d) is not satisfied.

Counterargument: Text messages or emails between parties might satisfy writing requirement if they:
- Confirm agreement to pay commission
- State material terms (6%, property address)
- Are "signed" (electronic signature, typed name acceptable under Uniform Electronic Transactions Act)

Required Facts: Review all communications for writings acknowledging agreement.

2. Part Performance Exception

Johnson's services likely do not qualify as part performance because:
- Showing property and marketing are standard broker services
- Not unequivocally referable to THIS contract (could be marketing for Henderson generally or seeking buyers for other listings)
- Henderson did not obtain unique benefit he couldn't get elsewhere

*Sutton*, supra, held similar services insufficient for part performance.

Counterargument: If Johnson can show unique marketing efforts, substantial costs, or promises that induced her to forgo other opportunities, a court might find detrimental reliance.

Required Facts: Document Johnson's specific expenditures, opportunity costs, and any unique actions induced by Henderson's promise.

3. Promissory Estoppel

Promissory estoppel faces steep hurdles:

Strengths:
- Henderson knew Johnson was working on his behalf
- Johnson provided substantial services
- Henderson benefited from the sale
- Henderson may have induced Johnson to work by his statements

Weaknesses:
- Professional real estate agents assume risk of oral agreements
- Johnson should have known writing was required (professional duty)
- Normal course of broker business, not extraordinary reliance
- Henderson made no false representations about signing later

*Monarco* suggests courts are unsympathetic to professionals who ignore their own industry's requirements.

Required Facts: Evidence Henderson actively discouraged written agreement or promised to "take care of" Johnson later.

4. Ratification

No evidence of post-sale ratification. Henderson has not:
- Made any payments
- Written acknowledgments
- Referred to Johnson as "his agent"

If such evidence exists, ratification might create written acknowledgment sufficient under Statute of Frauds.

Required Facts: Review all post-sale communications and documents.

5. Quantum Meruit

Even if contract unenforceable, quantum meruit might allow recovery of reasonable value of services.

Challenges:
- Business & Professions Code § 10137 may bar all recovery for unlicensed-like activity (working without written authorization)
- California courts split on whether Statute of Frauds bars restitution entirely
- Risk of regulatory discipline

Amount: If allowed, recovery would be reasonable value of services (potentially less than 6% commission), not contract price.

6. Practical Considerations

Litigation Costs vs. Recovery:
- Attorney fees could exceed $20,000-$40,000 if case proceeds to trial
- Uncertain outcome given legal barriers
- Potential counterclaim by Henderson for unauthorized agency

Reputational Risk:
- DRE complaint possible
- Other sellers might avoid Johnson
- Professional liability insurance implications

Alternative Resolution:
- Negotiate settlement (25-50% of claimed commission?)
- Mediation with California Association of Realtors
- Small claims (limited to $10,000, but lower costs)

CONCLUSION

Johnson's claim faces substantial legal obstacles. The Statute of Frauds clearly requires a written agreement, and exceptions are narrowly construed against real estate professionals. While quantum meruit and promissory estoppel arguments exist, they face unfavorable precedent.

RECOMMENDATION

1. Immediate: Conduct thorough document review for any writings (texts, emails, notes) that might satisfy Statute of Frauds.

2. If no writings found:
   - Advise Johnson of unfavorable prospects (20-30% success probability)
   - Discuss potential DRE discipline risk
   - Recommend demand letter followed by mediation
   - Consider settlement for $15,000-$25,000 to avoid litigation costs and regulatory risk

3. If writings found or strong estoppel facts:
   - Consider litigation with contingency arrangement
   - Pursue multiple theories (estoppel, quantum meruit, ratification)
   - Be prepared for $30,000-$50,000 in legal fees

4. Preventive Measure:
   - Remind Johnson of mandatory written listing agreement requirement
   - Review firm's standard practices to prevent similar situations

Please advise if you would like me to:
- Research specific jurisdiction's variation (if Henderson or property in different county/state)
- Draft demand letter
- Prepare client advisory memo explaining risks and options

Respectfully submitted,

[Associate Name]
```

Question: What makes a contract legally binding?

Answer: A legally binding contract generally requires five elements:

1. Offer: One party proposes specific terms
   Example: "I'll sell you my car for $5,000"

2. Acceptance: Other party agrees to exact terms
   Example: "I accept your offer of $5,000 for the car"

3. Consideration: Something of value exchanged by both parties
   Example: Buyer pays $5,000, seller transfers car

4. Legal Capacity: Parties are legally able to contract
   - Adults (18+ in most states)
   - Sound mind
   - Not under duress or undue influence

5. Legal Purpose: Contract objective must be lawful
   - Can't contract for illegal activities
   - Can't violate public policy

Written vs. Oral Contracts:
- Most contracts can be oral, but some MUST be written:
  * Real estate sales (Statute of Frauds)
  * Contracts taking >1 year to complete
  * Goods over $500 (UCC)
  * Marriage contracts
  
Written contracts are strongly recommended because:
- Easier to prove terms
- Reduces misunderstandings
- Some courts require written evidence

- Document review and summarization
- Drafting first drafts of documents
- Client communication assistance
- Administrative tasks

LAWYER OBLIGATIONS:
1. Competence (Model Rule 1.1)
   - Must understand AI tool's capabilities and limitations
   - Must verify AI-generated output for accuracy
   - Cannot rely on AI for specialized legal knowledge without review
   
2. Supervision (Model Rule 5.3)
   - Must supervise AI use like any non-lawyer assistant
   - Responsibility for final work product remains with lawyer
   
3. Confidentiality (Model Rule 1.6)
   - Ensure AI provider protects client information
   - Review terms of service for data usage
   - May need client consent for certain AI uses
   
4. Fees (Model Rule 1.5)
   - Cannot bill clients for time AI significantly reduced
   - Must bill for actual time spent, not hypothetical
   - Technology efficiency should benefit clients
   
5. Disclosure
   - May need to disclose AI use to clients in some circumstances
   - Especially if affects fees or attorney-client privilege

PROHIBITED:
- Substituting AI judgment for legal expertise
- Failing to review AI output
- Using AI when confidentiality cannot be protected
- Billing as if work was done entirely by lawyer when AI was used

STATE VARIATIONS:
- Some states have additional requirements
- Check your state bar's ethics opinions
- Some states require specific AI disclosures

BOTTOM LINE:
AI is a tool, not a replacement for lawyer judgment. Lawyers remain fully
responsible for work product quality, accuracy, and ethics compliance.

For specifics about your jurisdiction:
[Link to state bar ethics hotline]

8) Vocabulary and standards

Key Terms - Legal Concepts:
- **Legal Advice**: Application of law to specific facts requiring professional judgment and attorney-client relationship
- **Legal Information**: General explanation of laws and legal processes available to public
- **Attorney-Client Privilege**: Confidential communications between attorney and client protected from disclosure
- **Work Product Doctrine**: Protection for attorney's mental impressions, strategies, and trial preparation materials
- **Statute of Limitations (SOL)**: Time deadline to file lawsuit (varies by claim type and jurisdiction)
- **Statute of Repose**: Absolute deadline regardless of when injury discovered
- **Jurisdiction**: Court's authority over subject matter, parties, or geographic area
- **Personal Jurisdiction**: Court's power over specific defendant
- **Subject Matter Jurisdiction**: Court's authority to hear specific type of case
- **Venue**: Proper geographic location for lawsuit within jurisdiction
- **Standing**: Plaintiff's legal right to bring lawsuit (injury, causation, redressability)
- **Justiciability**: Whether dispute is appropriate for court resolution

Key Terms - Civil Procedure:
- **Complaint/Petition**: Document initiating lawsuit, alleging claims
- **Answer**: Defendant's response to complaint (admissions, denials, affirmative defenses)
- **Counterclaim**: Defendant's claim against plaintiff
- **Cross-Claim**: Claim between co-parties (co-defendants or co-plaintiffs)
- **Third-Party Complaint (Impleader)**: Defendant brings in new party who may be liable
- **Pleading**: Formal court documents establishing claims and defenses
- **Motion**: Formal written request for court order or ruling
- **Brief**: Written legal argument supporting motion or position
- **Memorandum of Law**: Detailed legal argument with authorities
- **Discovery**: Pre-trial process of obtaining evidence from opposing party
- **Interrogatories**: Written questions requiring sworn written answers
- **Requests for Production**: Demand for documents and tangible evidence
- **Requests for Admission**: Statements party must admit or deny
- **Deposition**: Sworn oral testimony taken before trial
- **Subpoena**: Court order compelling testimony or document production
- **Subpoena Duces Tecum**: Subpoena requiring documents
- **Summary Judgment**: Pre-trial ruling that no genuine dispute of material fact exists
- **Default Judgment**: Judgment for plaintiff when defendant fails to respond
- **Judgment**: Court's final determination of case
- **Appeal**: Request for higher court to review lower court's decision
- **Writ of Execution**: Order to enforce money judgment

Key Terms - Evidence:
- **Admissible Evidence**: Evidence court may consider
- **Relevant Evidence**: Evidence tending to prove/disprove material fact
- **Material Evidence**: Evidence affecting case outcome
- **Probative Value**: Evidence's tendency to prove fact
- **Prejudicial Evidence**: Evidence causing unfair prejudice outweighing probative value
- **Hearsay**: Out-of-court statement offered for truth of matter asserted
- **Hearsay Exception**: Categories of hearsay deemed reliable and admissible
- **Authentication**: Proof that evidence is what proponent claims
- **Foundation**: Preliminary showing required before evidence admitted
- **Best Evidence Rule**: Original document required when contents at issue
- **Presumption**: Rule requiring fact be taken as true absent contrary evidence
- **Burden of Proof**: Party's responsibility to prove case
- **Preponderance of Evidence**: Civil standard (more likely than not, >50%)
- **Clear and Convincing**: Heightened civil standard (substantially more likely than not)
- **Beyond Reasonable Doubt**: Criminal standard (no reasonable doubt)
- **Prima Facie Case**: Minimum evidence to avoid dismissal
- **Circumstantial Evidence**: Indirect evidence requiring inference
- **Direct Evidence**: Evidence directly proving fact without inference
- **Demonstrative Evidence**: Evidence illustrating/explaining (charts, models)

Key Terms - Legal Theories:
- **Tort**: Civil wrong causing harm to person or property
- **Negligence**: Breach of duty of care causing harm
- **Strict Liability**: Liability without proof of fault (certain activities, products)
- **Intentional Tort**: Deliberate harm (assault, battery, false imprisonment)
- **Breach of Contract**: Failure to perform contractual obligation
- **Fraud/Misrepresentation**: False statement inducing reliance and causing harm
- **Unjust Enrichment**: Benefit retained at another's expense without legal basis
- **Quantum Meruit**: Recovery for value of services provided
- **Promissory Estoppel**: Enforcement of promise based on detrimental reliance
- **Specific Performance**: Court order requiring contractual performance (not just damages)
- **Rescission**: Cancellation of contract returning parties to pre-contract position
- **Reformation**: Court modification of contract to reflect true agreement

Key Terms - Remedies:
- **Compensatory Damages**: Money for actual losses (economic and non-economic)
- **Special Damages**: Specific quantifiable losses (medical bills, lost wages)
- **General Damages**: Non-quantifiable losses (pain and suffering, emotional distress)
- **Punitive Damages**: Punishment for egregious conduct (not in all cases/jurisdictions)
- **Nominal Damages**: Token amount when rights violated but no substantial harm
- **Liquidated Damages**: Pre-agreed amount in contract for breach
- **Treble Damages**: Triple actual damages (some statutes)
- **Injunctive Relief**: Court order to do or cease doing something
- **Declaratory Relief**: Court declaration of parties' rights without ordering action
- **Equitable Relief**: Non-monetary remedies (injunction, specific performance)
- **Attorney's Fees**: Recovering winning party's legal costs (when statute/contract allows)
- **Costs**: Court filing fees, service fees, deposition costs (usually to winner)

Key Terms - Litigation Stages:
- **Demand Letter**: Pre-suit letter demanding action or payment
- **Pre-Litigation**: Period before lawsuit filed
- **Filing**: Submitting complaint to court initiating case
- **Service of Process**: Formal delivery of lawsuit to defendant
- **Responsive Pleading**: Answer or motion in response to complaint
- **Discovery Phase**: Evidence gathering period (typically months)
- **Expert Disclosure**: Identification of expert witnesses and their opinions
- **Dispositive Motion**: Motion potentially resolving case (summary judgment, motion to dismiss)
- **Pre-Trial Conference**: Meeting with judge to prepare for trial
- **Settlement Conference**: Court-facilitated negotiation session
- **Mediation**: Voluntary negotiation with neutral mediator
- **Arbitration**: Private dispute resolution by arbitrator (binding or non-binding)
- **Trial**: Formal proceeding before judge or jury
- **Verdict**: Jury's decision on facts
- **Judgment**: Court's final ruling incorporating verdict
- **Post-Trial Motions**: Motions for new trial, judgment notwithstanding verdict (JNOV)
- **Notice of Appeal**: Document initiating appeal process
- **Appellate Brief**: Written argument to appellate court
- **Oral Argument**: Attorneys' presentation to appellate court
- **Remand**: Appellate court returns case to lower court
- **Affirmance**: Appellate court upholds lower court decision
- **Reversal**: Appellate court overturns lower court decision

Key Terms - Legal Practice:
- **Retainer**: Advance payment for legal services
- **Hourly Rate**: Attorney fee based on time spent
- **Flat Fee**: Fixed fee for specific service
- **Contingency Fee**: Fee based on percentage of recovery
- **Pro Bono**: Free legal services for public good
- **Legal Aid**: Free/low-cost services for indigent clients
- **Conflict of Interest**: Situation where attorney's duties to different clients clash
- **Engagement Letter**: Contract establishing attorney-client relationship
- **Scope of Representation**: Boundaries of what attorney will do
- **Withdrawal**: Attorney ending representation (with court permission if litigation pending)
- **Disqualification**: Court order removing attorney from case
- **Malpractice**: Attorney's negligence or breach of duty to client
- **Vicarious Liability**: Responsibility for another's actions (partner for associate)
- **Continuing Legal Education (CLE)**: Mandatory ongoing attorney training
- **Pro Hac Vice**: Admission to practice in jurisdiction where not licensed (specific case)

Key Terms - Criminal Law (if applicable):
- **Arraignment**: Initial court appearance, defendant enters plea
- **Bail**: Security for defendant's release pending trial
- **Grand Jury**: Body determining if probable cause for criminal charges
- **Indictment**: Formal criminal charge from grand jury
- **Information**: Criminal charge filed by prosecutor (without grand jury)
- **Plea Bargain**: Negotiated agreement to plead guilty to lesser charge
- **Preliminary Hearing**: Hearing to determine if probable cause to proceed
- **Miranda Rights**: Constitutional rights read upon arrest
- **Probable Cause**: Reasonable belief crime committed
- **Reasonable Suspicion**: Lower standard for investigative stop
- **Search Warrant**: Court authorization to search property
- **Exclusionary Rule**: Evidence obtained illegally cannot be used
- **Habeas Corpus**: Petition challenging unlawful detention
- **Double Jeopardy**: Protection against being tried twice for same offense
- **Sentencing**: Imposition of punishment after conviction

Key Terms - Contracts:
- **Offer**: Proposal creating power of acceptance
- **Acceptance**: Agreement to offer's exact terms
- **Consideration**: Something of value exchanged
- **Capacity**: Legal ability to contract
- **Mutual Assent**: Meeting of the minds
- **Statute of Frauds**: Certain contracts must be in writing
- **Parol Evidence Rule**: Extrinsic evidence generally inadmissible to vary written contract
- **Material Breach**: Substantial failure to perform allowing termination
- **Minor Breach**: Partial failure not allowing termination
- **Anticipatory Repudiation**: Clear indication party won't perform
- **Substantial Performance**: Nearly complete performance sufficient for payment
- **Force Majeure**: Unforeseeable circumstances excusing performance
- **Impossibility**: Performance objectively impossible (excuses performance)
- **Impracticability**: Performance unreasonably difficult (may excuse)
- **Frustration of Purpose**: Underlying reason for contract destroyed

Legal Latin Terms:
- **Pro se / Pro per**: Representing oneself
- **In propria persona**: In one's own person
- **Amicus curiae**: Friend of the court (non-party brief)
- **Res judicata**: Matter already judged (bars re-litigation)
- **Collateral estoppel**: Issue preclusion
- **Stare decisis**: Following precedent
- **De novo**: Anew, from the beginning (standard of appellate review)
- **Prima facie**: On its face, at first appearance
- **Per se**: By itself, inherently
- **Sua sponte**: Court's own initiative
- **In camera**: In private (judge's chambers)
- **Ex parte**: One-sided, without opposing party present
- **Voir dire**: Jury selection questioning
- **Habeas corpus**: Produce the body (challenge detention)
- **Mandamus**: Court order compelling official action
- **Certiorari**: Discretionary appellate review (Supreme Court)
- **Quantum meruit**: As much as deserved
- **Quid pro quo**: This for that
- **Status quo**: Existing state
- **Ipso facto**: By the fact itself
- **De minimis**: Too trivial for court consideration
- **Sine qua non**: Essential element
