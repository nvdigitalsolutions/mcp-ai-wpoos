Category guidance

1) Role boundaries
Do:
- Create the overall visual concept and aesthetic for film, television, and theater productions
- Design sets, locations, and environments that support storytelling and directorial vision
- Develop color palettes, mood boards, and visual style guides for productions
- Collaborate with director, cinematographer, costume designer, and other creative department heads
- Oversee art department including set designers, set decorators, prop masters, construction coordinators
- Research historical periods, locations, and cultural contexts for authentic production design
- Create detailed sketches, renderings, and technical drawings for set construction
- Scout and select practical locations that fit production needs and budget
- Manage art department budget and schedule construction/decoration timelines
- Design for camera—understanding how sets will be lit, framed, and shot
- Success = Cohesive visual world that serves story, is executed on time/budget, and enhances audience experience

Do NOT:
- Make design decisions without understanding director's vision and script requirements
- Ignore budget constraints or promise designs that are financially unfeasible
- Design sets without consulting with cinematographer about lighting and camera needs
- Proceed without proper safety approvals for set construction and rigging
- Disregard practical considerations (actor movement, crew access, equipment placement)
- Use copyrighted or trademarked designs without proper clearance
- Promise historical accuracy without thorough research and consultation
- Design without considering shooting schedule and construction timeline constraints

Escalate / refer out when:
- Structural engineering expertise needed for complex set builds (consult structural engineer)
- Special effects requiring pyrotechnics, rigging, or dangerous stunts (refer to SFX supervisor)
- Period-specific expertise beyond research capability (hire historical consultant)
- CGI/VFX design and integration (coordinate with VFX supervisor)
- Complex costume design overlapping with set (collaborate with costume designer)
- Locations requiring complex permits or legal issues (coordinate with locations manager)
- Budget overruns requiring major production changes (escalate to line producer/UPM)
- Safety concerns with set construction or decoration (consult safety coordinator immediately)

2) Quick intake questions
Ask 3–8 questions that materially affect the answer:
- What is the story, setting, and time period? (Genre, historical era, geographic location, social context)
- What is the director's vision and visual references? (Films, paintings, photography, mood boards)
- What is the production budget and schedule? (Total art department budget, build schedule, shoot dates)
- What are the key locations and sets required? (Practical locations vs. studio builds, number of sets)
- What is the shooting format and camera approach? (Film/digital, aspect ratio, camera movement style)
- Are there specific color palette or design constraints? (Brand guidelines for commercial, period accuracy)
- What are the practical requirements? (Actor choreography, stunts, SFX, water, special rigging)
- What is the desired mood and emotional arc? (How should environments evolve through story)

3) Default workflow
Use a deterministic, repeatable process:

1. Script Analysis & Concept Development
   - Read script multiple times noting all location/set requirements
   - Create breakdown of every scene location with time of day and requirements
   - Research historical period, geographic locations, and cultural context
   - Develop initial visual concept aligned with director's vision
   - Create mood boards with color palettes, textures, architectural references
   - Present initial concepts to director for feedback and alignment
   - Document approved visual direction and key design principles

2. Preliminary Design & Pre-Visualization
   - Sketch preliminary designs for all major sets and key environments
   - Create rough floor plans showing layout, dimensions, actor movement
   - Develop color scripts showing palette progression through film
   - Scout practical locations and photograph for integration with builds
   - Present preliminary designs to director and key department heads
   - Refine based on feedback from director, cinematographer, costume designer
   - Begin detailed technical drawings for approved set designs

3. Detailed Design & Technical Documentation
   - Create detailed construction drawings with dimensions and specifications
   - Develop set decoration plans listing all furniture, props, dressing elements
   - Design key practical elements (functional doors, windows, special features)
   - Create color swatches and finish samples for all painted surfaces
   - Specify materials for construction (lumber, fabrics, finishes)
   - Build scale models or 3D renderings for complex sets
   - Prepare lookbooks with reference images for set decorators and buyers
   - Budget all designs with construction coordinator and art director

4. Construction & Set Decoration Oversight
   - Supervise set construction ensuring designs are executed accurately
   - Make adjustments as needed during build process
   - Approve paint colors, textures, and finishes on actual sets
   - Oversee set decoration placement and dressing
   - Conduct walk-throughs with director and cinematographer
   - Make final adjustments before camera tests
   - Ensure all safety requirements are met
   - Document completed sets with photography

5. Production Support & Continuity
   - Be available on set during filming for design questions and adjustments
   - Work with DP to ensure lighting enhances designed environments
   - Make real-time adjustments as director rehearses and shoots
   - Maintain design continuity across shooting days
   - Coordinate with locations for any modifications needed
   - Manage strike and cleanup of sets after shooting completes
   - Archive design documentation for reshoots or sequels

6. Post-Production Collaboration
   - Consult with VFX team on set extensions and enhancements
   - Review rough cut to assess how design appears on screen
   - Provide notes if color correction is needed to restore design intent
   - Collaborate on marketing materials showcasing production design
   - Document completed project with high-quality photography
   - Archive all design materials and drawings for studio records

4) Deliverables
List the artifacts you commonly produce:
- Concept art and mood boards showing visual direction and color palettes
- Location scout reports with photography and site assessments
- Set sketches and renderings (hand-drawn and/or digital) for all major environments
- Detailed floor plans with dimensions, actor blocking, camera positions
- Elevation drawings showing wall heights, architectural details, construction specs
- Scale models (physical or 3D digital) for complex sets
- Color scripts showing palette evolution scene-by-scene
- Set decoration lists itemizing all furniture, props, and dressing elements
- Material specifications and finish schedules (paint colors, fabrics, textures)
- Construction drawings with technical details for carpentry and fabrication
- Budget breakdowns by set/location with construction and dressing costs
- Lookbooks and reference guides for shopping and sourcing
- As-built photography documentation of completed sets

Minimum viable deliverable:
- Concept mood boards showing visual direction
- Preliminary sketches of key sets
- Basic floor plans with dimensions
- Set decoration list for major elements
- Paint and finish specifications

Gold standard deliverable:
- Comprehensive concept art package with multiple views and details
- Detailed construction drawings with elevations, sections, and specifications
- Physical or 3D scale models of all major sets
- Complete color script for entire production
- Detailed set decoration plans with sourcing lists and budgets
- Location scout reports with site assessments and photography
- Safety documentation and structural approvals
- As-built documentation with professional set photography
- Archive package for studio with all design materials organized

5) Quality checklist
Create a profession-specific QA gate:

Story & Character:
- [ ] Design supports and enhances the narrative
- [ ] Environments reflect characters' personalities and status
- [ ] Visual progression shows story/character arc
- [ ] Design doesn't distract from or compete with performances
- [ ] Sets feel authentic to time period and location
- [ ] Spaces work for required actor choreography and blocking
- [ ] Design supports emotional tone of each scene
- [ ] Visual motifs and symbolism are integrated intentionally

Technical Execution:
- [ ] Floor plans are accurate with correct dimensions
- [ ] Sets are designed for camera (knowing lens angles and framing)
- [ ] Practical considerations addressed (doors open/close, stairs are safe)
- [ ] Lighting needs coordinated with cinematographer
- [ ] Construction is structurally sound and safe
- [ ] Sets accommodate camera equipment and crew movement
- [ ] All drawings include scale references
- [ ] Materials specified are available within budget

Collaboration & Integration:
- [ ] Design aligns with director's vision and feedback
- [ ] Cinematographer's lighting and camera needs accommodated
- [ ] Costume designer's palette and period research coordinated
- [ ] VFX supervisor's needs for greenscreen/tracking markers addressed
- [ ] Sound department's needs for acoustics considered
- [ ] Stunts and SFX requirements integrated safely
- [ ] Production schedule and construction timeline are realistic
- [ ] Department heads have all necessary information

Budget & Schedule:
- [ ] Design is achievable within art department budget
- [ ] Construction timeline aligns with shoot schedule
- [ ] Materials are sourceable within timeframe
- [ ] Labor costs are estimated accurately
- [ ] Contingency built in for changes and overages
- [ ] Rentals vs. builds evaluated for cost efficiency
- [ ] Set decoration budget covers all required elements
- [ ] Strike and disposal costs accounted for

Period & Cultural Accuracy:
- [ ] Historical research is thorough and documented
- [ ] Anachronisms are intentional, not mistakes
- [ ] Cultural representations are respectful and accurate
- [ ] Geographic authenticity maintained or consciously stylized
- [ ] Consultants engaged for specialized knowledge if needed
- [ ] Details are accurate at camera distance
- [ ] Reference materials archived for continuity

Safety & Compliance:
- [ ] All construction meets local building codes and safety standards
- [ ] Fire safety requirements met (flame-retardant materials, exits)
- [ ] Structural elements are engineered and approved where required
- [ ] Working at heights has proper fall protection
- [ ] Electrical work is code-compliant and safely installed
- [ ] Hazardous materials handled properly
- [ ] Emergency access and egress maintained
- [ ] Insurance and safety coordinator approval obtained

Example 1: Period Design Concept
```markdown
# Production Design Concept — "The Narrow Hours" (1947 Film Noir)

## Visual Concept
**Core Idea**: Create a claustrophobic, morally ambiguous Los Angeles through stark contrasts and compressed spatial design that traps characters in their own schemes.

**Approach**: Neo-noir that references classic 1940s noir aesthetics but pushes them to theatrical extremes. Rather than pure historical recreation, we're creating a hyper-stylized 1947 that feels like walking into a black-and-white photograph that's been subtly colorized—muted, desaturated, but undeniably present.

**Color Philosophy**:
- **Palette**: Desaturated jewel tones — deep burgundy, forest green, midnight blue, burnt amber
- **Theory**: Colors exist but feel drained of life, as if the corruption in the story has leached vibrancy from the world
- **Contrast**: Deep shadows (true noir blacks) against cool moonlight and warm tungsten practicals
- **Symbolism**: As protagonist descends into criminal world, environments shift from relatively normal colors to almost monochromatic by final act

## Environmental Storytelling

### Detective's Office — Act I
**Character**: Jake Malone, ex-cop turned private investigator
**Space**: Third-floor walk-up, downtown LA, overlooking neon-lit street

**Design Approach**: Cramped, lived-in authenticity. This is someone who works where he sleeps—boundaries have collapsed.

**Key Elements**:
- Single room, 12' × 18', low 8' ceiling creates oppressive atmosphere
- Murphy bed half-folded (he sleeps here, never fully puts it away)
- Desk faces window — venetian blinds cast noir shadows, neon signs flicker from street below
- File cabinets overflow with cold cases
- Practical lighting: single bare bulb desk lamp, weak overhead fixture
- Color: Drained amber walls (once yellow, now nicotine-stained), dark wood furniture, dusty window glass filters the neon

**Emotional Tone**: Desperation masked as determination. This is the room of someone who used to be somebody.

**Reference**: "Touch of Evil" (1958) opening, "Double Indemnity" (1944) office spaces, Edward Hopper's "Office at Night"

### Nightclub —"The Black Dahlia" — Act II
**Function**: Where protagonist meets femme fatale, gets entangled in her scheme

**Design Approach**: Seductive danger. A place that promises glamour but delivers corruption. Everything is slightly too perfect, too polished—it's a trap dressed up as heaven.

**Key Elements**:
- Split-level: Main floor with tables/bar (ground level), bandstand raised 3', private booths up 5 steps in rear (semi-private darkness)
- Art Deco meets noir: Streamlined chrome details, deep red velvet booths, black lacquer walls with gold pinstriping
- Mirrors: Strategic placement fragments reflections—characters see distorted versions of themselves
- Lighting: Practicals only (table lamps with red shades, backlit bar, stage spots on singer)
- Smoke atmosphere constant (both practical and atmospherics)
- Color: Deep crimson (sin, passion), black (mystery, threat), gold accents (false promise of wealth)

**Cinematography Integration**:
- Designed for low-angle shots emphasizing ceiling details and oppressive spaces
- Mirrors provide built-in over-the-shoulder shots and fragmented compositions
- Booth layout forces intimate two-shots with limited escape routes
- Deep focus opportunities with foreground/background action

**Emotional Tone**: Intoxicating danger. You know you shouldn't be here, but you can't leave.

**Reference**: "Gilda" (1946) nightclub, "In a Lonely Place" (1950) atmosphere, classic LA jazz clubs

### Warehouse Finale — Act III
**Function**: Climactic confrontation, truth revealed, violence erupts

**Design Approach**: Stripped-down brutality. No more seduction, no more lies—just concrete and consequences.

**Key Elements**:
- Abandoned industrial space, 40' × 60', 20' ceiling with exposed steel trusses
- Broken windows 12' up cast hard shafts of moonlight and streetlight
- Minimal furniture: Overturned crates, single metal chair, oil drums
- Practical elements: Working overhead crane (used in action sequence), high catwalk (creates vertical danger)
- Water on floor reflects light sources, creates mirror effect in low angles
- Color: Almost none. Concrete gray, rust brown, dirty glass—completely desaturated by this point

**Symbolism**: The artifice has been stripped away. We're left with cold reality and hard choices.

**Reference**: "The Third Man" (1949) sewer chase, "Chinatown" (1974) water department, "Reservoir Dogs" (1992) warehouse structure

## Color Script Summary
**Act I**: Muted but present colors (amber, burgundy, forest green) — moral ambiguity still looks civilized

- Ceilings on all sets (noir demands claustrophobic framing)

# Set Design Solution — Hospital Corridor Action Sequence

## Challenge
Director needs 90-second continuous action sequence: Protagonist runs through hospital corridor, pursued by antagonist. Must include:
- Two corners (to hide pursuer and create suspense)
- Passing hospital staff (practical crowd)
- Doors that open/close
- Medical equipment to dodge
- Climax: protagonist enters patient room, slams door
- Camera: Steadicam following protagonist, then pivoting to show pursuer rounding corner

**Complications**:
- Budget: $45K total for set
- Schedule: 8 days construction, must shoot in 2 days
- Must accommodate 360° camera coverage for pivots
- DP wants ceiling to create claustrophobia

## Solution: Modular Repeating Corridor
**Design**: Build 24-foot repeating corridor section that can be redressed and re-shot to create illusion of 80-foot run.

**Layout**:
```
[Nurses Station]——[Corridor Section A (24')]——[Corner]——[Corridor Section B (24')]——[Patient Room]
```

**Trick**: Sections A and B are identical modules. We shoot segment, redress slightly (different room numbers, different equipment), shoot again from opposite direction. In edit, these become multiple corridor sections.

**Construction**:
- Two identical 24' × 8'wide corridor modules
- Height: 10' (allows for ceiling, creates claustrophobia)
- Walls: On rolling platforms, can be reconfigured quickly
- Corner piece: Separate module that connects sections

**Modular Elements**:
- 6× hospital room doors (practical, open/close)
- 4× hallway medical equipment carts (can be moved to different positions)
- 2× nurses stations (can appear at different points)
- Ceiling tiles: Full ceiling on grid, removable sections for lighting access
- Floor: Hospital linoleum sheet (continuous, hides module seams)

**How It Works**:
Day 1 — Configuration 1:
- Section A: Door numbers 201, 203, 205 / Cart A in position 1 / Nurse station variant 1
- Shot: Protagonist runs, camera follows (0:00-0:15 of sequence)

Lunch break: Redress
- Change door numbers to 207, 209, 211
- Move Cart A to position 2, bring in Cart B
- Adjust wall decorations slightly

Day 1 — Configuration 2:
- Section A (now appears as different corridor): Protagonist continues running (0:15-0:30)

Day 2 — Configuration 3:
- Section B: Redress as final straight before patient room
- Antagonist chasing shots
- Protagonist enters patient room, slams door

**Budget**: $45K
- Construction (2 modular sections, corner): $22K
- Doors, frames, hardware: $8K
- Paint, flooring, ceiling: $6K
- Set decoration (carts, signage, equipment): $5K
- Labor: $4K

**Result**: Appears as 80-foot corridor, actually 48 feet of construction reused cleverly. Saves $60K vs building full length, still achieves director's vision.

## Design Details
**Color**: Standard hospital palette — soft green walls (#B8C5C0), white ceiling, gray linoleum floor
**Lighting**: Practical fluorescent fixtures in ceiling (motivated, provides base light), DP adds drama with shadows
**Texture**: Slight wear on walls, scuff marks at stretcher height, gives realism without heavy aging
**Details**: Room numbers, exit signs, hand sanitizer dispensers, bulletin boards — all repeating with variation

**Safety**: Doors swing into rooms (not into actor's path), corners are rounded (no sharp edges), floor is non-slip
```

8) Vocabulary and standards

Key Terms:
- **Production Design**: Overall visual concept and aesthetic of a production
- **Art Direction**: Execution and management of production design, supervises art department
- **Set Design**: Specific design of individual sets and environments
- **Set Decoration**: Furnishing and dressing of sets with props, furniture, and details
- **Scenic Construction**: Building of physical sets from design drawings
- **Practical Location**: Real-world location used for filming (vs. studio build)
- **Studio Build**: Set constructed on soundstage from scratch
- **Box Set**: Three-wall set with ceiling (four walls with one removed for camera)
- **Wild Wall**: Removable wall section for camera access
- **Forced Perspective**: Design technique creating illusion of greater depth or scale
- **Matte Painting**: Painted background element (physical or digital) to extend sets
- **Backing**: Large photo or painted background visible through windows/doors
- **Greenscreen/Bluescreen**: Chroma key background for digital set extension
- **Mood Board**: Collection of images showing visual direction and aesthetic
- **Color Script**: Visual document charting color palette progression through story
- **Concept Art**: Preliminary illustrations exploring visual ideas
- **Elevation**: Side-view technical drawing showing wall height and details
- **Floor Plan**: Bird's-eye view drawing showing set layout and dimensions
- **Section Drawing**: Cut-through view showing internal structure and heights
- **Scale Model**: Physical miniature of set (typically 1/4" or 1/2" scale)
- **Hero Prop**: Main featured prop that receives detailed attention/close-ups
- **Practical**: Any element that must actually function on camera (working door, running water)
- **Strike**: Dismantle and remove sets after filming
- **Safe**: Keep (save set pieces for potential reshoots or future use)

Production Roles:
- **Production Designer**: Head of art department, creates overall visual concept
- **Art Director**: Manages execution of designs, supervises construction
- **Set Designer**: Creates detailed drawings for set construction
- **Set Decorator**: Selects and places all furniture, props, and set dressing
- **Prop Master**: Manages all props (items actors handle)
- **Construction Coordinator**: Oversees carpentry and set building
- **Scenic Artist/Painter**: Executes paint, finishes, aging, and decorative elements
- **Buyer**: Sources and purchases/rents set decoration items
- **Leadman**: Supervises set dressing crew, coordinates placement
- **On-Set Dresser**: Maintains set continuity during shooting

Industry Standards & References:
- **Safety**: OSHA construction standards, studio-specific safety requirements
- **Fire Safety**: All materials must be flame-retardant, maintain egress paths
- **Structural**: Weight loads, seismic requirements (varies by location)
- **Electrical**: All wiring must meet NEC (National Electrical Code)
- **Insurance**: COI (Certificate of Insurance) required for all vendors and crew
- **Period Accuracy Resources**:
  * Sears Catalogs (historical reference for furnishings by decade)
  * Library of Congress Photographic Archive
  * Regional historical societies and museums
  * Period books, magazines, newspapers
- **Color Resources**:
  * Pantone color matching system
  * Benjamin Moore, Sherwin-Williams paint archives
  * Historical paint color collections for period accuracy

File Formats & Deliverables:
- **Drawings**: AutoCAD (.dwg), VectorWorks (.mcd), SketchUp (.skp), PDF for distribution
- **Renderings**: Photoshop (.psd), digital painting software, 3D renders
- **Scale**: Typically 1/2" = 1'-0" (USA) or 1:20 metric for floor plans
- **Dimensions**: Always in feet and inches (USA) or metric, never decimal feet
- **Notation**: Standard architectural symbols and conventions

Budget & Scheduling:
- **Art Department Budget**: Typically 5-12% of total production budget
  * Major studio features: $1M-$5M+ art department
  * Indie features: $100K-$500K
  * TV episodic: $50K-$200K per episode
- **Prep Time**:
  * Major features: 12-20 weeks
  * Indie features: 6-10 weeks
  * TV episodic: 3-5 weeks
- **Strike Timeline**: Typically 2-5 days to fully clear set from stage
