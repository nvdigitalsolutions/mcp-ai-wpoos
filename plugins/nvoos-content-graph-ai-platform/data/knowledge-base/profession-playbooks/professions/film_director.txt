Intent
- Provide actionable guidance for film direction following DGA/industry standards.
- Focus on visual storytelling, crew leadership, creative vision, and production collaboration.

Category guidance
- Creative vision leadership, collaboration, technical execution, practical production constraints.

1) Role boundaries
Do:
- Develop creative vision and visual storytelling approach for film/TV projects
- Collaborate with screenwriter to refine script and story structure
- Lead casting process and direct actor performances
- Design shot lists, storyboards, and blocking for scenes
- Work with cinematographer to establish visual style and camera work
- Guide editing process and post-production creative decisions
- Collaborate with producers on budget and schedule constraints
- Lead creative team (cinematographer, production designer, costume, sound)
- Success = Compelling visual narrative that serves the story and engages audience

Do NOT:
- Make financial decisions without producer approval (budget authority)
- Guarantee box office success or audience reception
- Violate union rules or safety protocols on set
- Ignore script supervisor continuity notes (creates editing problems)
- Promise actors specific edit outcomes (final cut usually producer/studio decision)
- Exceed schedule without producer agreement (financial liability)
- Direct without proper insurance and permits

Escalate / refer out when:
- Safety concerns on set requiring stunt coordinator or safety officer
- Legal issues with locations, permits, or intellectual property
- Budget overruns requiring studio/producer executive decision
- Cast or crew conflicts requiring mediation or HR intervention
- Technical challenges beyond crew capabilities (VFX supervisor, specialty equipment)
- Distribution or marketing decisions (studio/producer domain)

2) Quick intake questions
- What is the genre and format (feature, short, episodic TV, documentary, commercial)?
- What is the budget tier and schedule (micro-budget indie vs studio production)?
- Who is the target audience and distribution platform?
- What is the script status (locked, in development, shooting draft)?
- Who are the key creative collaborators (cinematographer, production designer)?
- What is the visual style or reference films inspiring the approach?
- What are the production constraints (locations, cast availability, weather)?
- What is the post-production timeline and workflow?

3) Default workflow

1. Pre-Production Development
   - Script analysis: Break down story structure, character arcs, themes
   - Create director's treatment: Visual approach and creative vision document
   - Develop shot list and storyboards for key sequences
   - Collaborate with department heads: Cinematographer, production designer, costume
   - Scout locations and plan camera placement
   - Conduct casting: Auditions, callbacks, chemistry reads
   - Rehearse with actors: Table reads, blocking rehearsals
   - Create shooting schedule with 1st AD (prioritize scenes by location/cast)
   - Technical prep: Camera tests, lens choices, lighting plans

2. Production (Principal Photography)
   - Morning preparation: Review shot list, discuss day's scenes with key crew
   - Blocking: Work with actors and cinematographer to stage scenes
   - Rehearsal: Run through blocking, refine performance choices
   - Lighting setup: Collaborate with cinematographer while actors prepare
   - Multiple takes: Coverage (master, mediums, close-ups, inserts)
   - Performance direction: Adjust actor choices, try variations
   - Monitor continuity: Work with script supervisor on matching
   - Review footage: Check playback for technical and performance quality
   - Adjust schedule: Adapt to weather, actor availability, time constraints
   - Daily communication: Update producers on progress and issues

3. Post-Production
   - Editor's assembly: Review first cut of sequences
   - Director's cut: Refine pacing, performance takes, story clarity
   - Collaborate on sound design: Music, dialogue, effects
   - VFX supervision: Review shots, approve final composites
   - Color correction: Work with colorist to establish visual tone
   - Test screenings: Gather audience feedback, consider adjustments
   - Final cut: Deliver approved version to producers/studio
   - Deliverables: DCP, ProRes files, various formats for distribution

4. Collaboration & Communication
   - Daily collaboration with cinematographer on visual approach
   - Regular producer check-ins on schedule and budget
   - Actor notes: Private, constructive feedback on performance
   - Crew leadership: Clear communication, respect for expertise
   - Problem-solving: Creative solutions within budget/schedule
   - Maintain creative vision while adapting to production realities

5. Professional Development
   - Watch diverse films: Study craft, analyze techniques
   - Study acting: Understand performance process
   - Technical knowledge: Camera, lighting, editing workflows
   - Networking: Build relationships with crew and industry professionals
   - Reel maintenance: Curate best work for future opportunities

4) Deliverables
- Director's treatment (visual approach document)
- Shot lists and storyboards
- Casting notes and actor direction
- Daily progress reports (to producers)
- Director's cut (post-production)
- Completed film/episode ready for distribution

Minimum viable: Clear shot list, effective actor direction, completed edit meeting story goals
Gold standard: Comprehensive storyboards, nuanced performances, cohesive visual style, efficient production

5) Quality checklist

Pre-Production:
- [ ] Script thoroughly analyzed (story, character, theme)
- [ ] Visual approach clearly defined
- [ ] Key creative collaborators aligned on vision
- [ ] Casting complete and actors rehearsed
- [ ] Locations scouted and shot list prepared
- [ ] Schedule realistic and approved by producers

Production:
- [ ] Coverage adequate for editing (master, mediums, close-ups)
- [ ] Performances meet creative vision
- [ ] Continuity maintained across takes and setups
- [ ] Safety protocols followed on all set activities
- [ ] Schedule maintained or variances communicated
- [ ] Collaboration effective with all department heads

Post-Production:
- [ ] Edit serves story and maintains pacing
- [ ] Sound design enhances emotional impact
- [ ] Visual effects integrated seamlessly
- [ ] Color grade supports visual tone
- [ ] Final cut approved by stakeholders
- [ ] Deliverables meet technical specifications

7) Examples of excellent outputs

Example: Director's Shot List for Tense Confrontation Scene

SHOT LIST - Scene 42: Kitchen Confrontation
Location: Sarah's Apartment Kitchen
Description: Sarah confronts Tom about the lie. Tension builds to emotional breaking point.

Shot 1: WIDE MASTER - LOCKED OFF
Frame: Both characters in frame, kitchen island between them creating visual barrier
Lens: 35mm (wider to emphasize physical distance)
Action: Full scene play-through, Sarah enters frame left, Tom already at island
Notes: Let actors play full emotional arc, capture geography for editing

Shot 2: MEDIUM - SARAH - HANDHELD
Frame: Waist up, slightly off-center (rule of thirds)
Lens: 50mm
Action: Sarah's entrance and first accusations
Notes: Slight handheld movement reflects emotional instability, follow as she moves

Shot 3: MEDIUM - TOM - LOCKED OFF
Frame: Waist up, centered, constrained by frame
Lens: 50mm
Action: Tom's defensive reactions
Notes: Static frame emphasizes his trapped feeling vs Sarah's movement

Shot 4: CLOSE-UP - SARAH - SLOW PUSH IN
Frame: Start at eyes, end tight on full face
Lens: 85mm
Action: "I trusted you" monologue
Notes: Slow dolly push increases emotional intensity, actor should build to tears

Shot 5: CLOSE-UP - TOM - LOCKED OFF
Frame: Tight on face, uncomfortable proximity
Lens: 85mm
Action: Tom's realization and attempt to explain
Notes: No camera movement—let performance carry the moment

Shot 6: INSERT - HANDS
Frame: Close on Sarah's hands gripping counter edge, knuckles white
Lens: 85mm macro
Action: Physical manifestation of tension
Notes: B-roll for editing, captures subtext

Shot 7: OTS (OVER TOM'S SHOULDER) - SARAH
Frame: Shallow focus, Tom's shoulder/back of head in foreground, Sarah in focus background
Lens: 85mm (1.4 for shallow DOF)
Action: Sarah's final decision to leave
Notes: Emphasizes emotional disconnection through focus

Coverage Checklist:
☑ Master (Shot 1)
☑ Medium shots (Shots 2-3)
☑ Close-ups (Shots 4-5)
☑ Insert (Shot 6)
☑ OTS (Shot 7)
☐ Reverse OTS (if time permits)

DIRECTOR NOTES:
- Scene is about trust broken and emotional walls going up
- Use camera distance to mirror emotional distance
- Sarah has movement (agency), Tom is trapped (static frames)
- Build from wide to tight as emotion intensifies
- Leave space for silence—don't rush coverage
- Watch for continuity on coffee mug and phone placement

8) Vocabulary and standards

Key Terms:
- **Coverage**: Multiple camera angles/shot sizes of same scene for editing options
- **Master Shot**: Wide shot establishing geography and action of entire scene
- **Blocking**: Choreography of actor and camera movement within a scene
- **Beat**: Smallest unit of action/emotion; pause in dialogue or shift in objective
- **Subtext**: Unspoken meaning beneath dialogue and action
- **Continuity**: Consistency of details across shots and takes for seamless editing
- **DCP**: Digital Cinema Package - standard format for theatrical distribution
- **Dailies**: Raw footage viewed shortly after shooting to check quality
- **Lock**: Finalizing script, edit, or other element (no more changes)

Standards & Frameworks:
- **DGA**: Directors Guild of America - professional union standards and protections
- **180-Degree Rule**: Imaginary line actors establish; camera stays on one side for spatial continuity
- **30-Degree Rule**: Camera should move at least 30 degrees between cuts to same subject
- **Three-Act Structure**: Setup, Confrontation, Resolution (classic screenplay structure)
- **Hero's Journey**: Joseph Campbell mythological story structure (departure, initiation, return)
- **Mise-en-scène**: Everything visible in frame (production design, lighting, blocking, costume)
- **Auteur Theory**: Director as author with consistent visual and thematic style

Common Formats:
- Feature Film: 90-180 minutes, theatrical or streaming release
- Episodic TV: 22min (network sitcom), 43min (network drama), 60min (prestige streaming)
- Short Film: Under 40 minutes (typically 10-15 for festival circuits)
- Web Series: 3-10 minutes per episode
- Commercial: 15, 30, or 60 seconds

Production Roles:
- **1st AD**: First Assistant Director (schedule, logistics, set management)
- **Script Supervisor**: Continuity, notes, timing
- **Cinematographer/DP**: Director of Photography, camera and lighting
- **Gaffer**: Chief lighting technician
- **Key Grip**: Rigging and camera support equipment
- **Production Designer**: Visual design of sets and locations
- **Sound Mixer**: Production sound recording

Post-Production Roles:
- **Editor**: Assembly and pacing of footage
- **Sound Designer**: Creating sonic landscape
- **Composer**: Original music score
- **Colorist**: Color grading and visual tone
- **VFX Supervisor**: Visual effects oversight and compositing
