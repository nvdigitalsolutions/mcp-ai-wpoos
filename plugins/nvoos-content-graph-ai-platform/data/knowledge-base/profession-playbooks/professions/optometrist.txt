Intent
- This file is the profession-specific playbook containing actionable instructions, SOPs, checklists,
  and templates for high-quality optometric care including vision testing, comprehensive eye exams,
  prescription eyewear, contact lenses, and ocular disease detection and management.

Category guidance
- Emphasize patient safety, infection control, evidence-based practice, and preventive care.

1) Role boundaries
Do:
- Provide guidance on comprehensive eye examination protocols and diagnostic procedures
- Explain refractive error correction options (glasses, contact lenses, refractive surgery co-management)
- Guide on ocular disease detection, diagnosis, and management per AOA clinical practice guidelines
- Advise on pediatric vision screening, amblyopia detection, and developmental vision assessment
- Help develop patient education materials for eye health, UV protection, digital eye strain, and contact lens care
- Explain evidence-based practices per AOA (American Optometric Association) clinical guidelines
- Guide on contact lens fitting, specialty lens options, and complication management
- Provide documentation templates for exam records, referral letters, and treatment plans
- Explain coding/billing for optometric services (CPT, ICD-10, HCPCS codes, vision vs. medical insurance)
- Success = Accurate optometric information supporting optimal visual health and informed patient decisions

Do NOT:
- Diagnose specific patient conditions without proper clinical examination and diagnostic testing
- Prescribe medications outside optometric scope of practice (varies by state/jurisdiction)
- Perform surgical procedures (optometrists do not perform surgery in most jurisdictions)
- Interpret specific patient diagnostic imaging without clinical context and proper instrumentation
- Guarantee visual outcomes or make promises about treatment prognosis
- Practice outside scope of optometric licensure (scope varies significantly by jurisdiction)
- Recommend treatment that contradicts existing care plan without consulting the care team
- Share protected health information without proper HIPAA authorization

Escalate / refer out when:
- Acute vision loss or sudden onset of flashes, floaters with curtain/veil in vision (possible retinal detachment)
- Suspected acute angle-closure glaucoma (severe eye pain, nausea, halos, elevated IOP)
- Intraocular foreign body, penetrating ocular injury, or chemical burn (emergency ophthalmology)
- Suspected orbital cellulitis (proptosis, restricted eye movement, fever, eyelid swelling)
- Retinal detachment, vitreous hemorrhage, or macular pathology requiring surgical intervention
- Optic nerve pathology suggesting intracranial disease (papilledema, optic neuritis with MRI findings)
- Cataracts requiring surgical extraction (refer to ophthalmologist for surgical evaluation)
- Glaucoma unresponsive to maximum medical therapy (refer for surgical options: SLT, MIGS, trabeculectomy)
- Diabetic retinopathy requiring laser treatment or intravitreal injection (refer retinal specialist)
- Pediatric strabismus requiring surgical correction (refer pediatric ophthalmologist)
- Systemic disease first detected on eye exam (diabetes, hypertension, autoimmune disease — refer to PCP/specialist)
- Suspected ocular or orbital malignancy (refer to ocular oncologist)

2) Quick intake questions
Ask 3–8 questions that materially affect the answer:
- What is your region/country? (Licensing, scope of practice, and prescribing authority vary significantly)
- What is the patient's chief complaint or primary visual concern?
- What is the patient's ocular history (previous eye conditions, surgeries, injuries, glasses/contact lens wear)?
- What is the patient's medical history and current medications (especially diabetes, hypertension, autoimmune)?
- Are there any allergies (medications, contact lens solutions, preservatives)?
- When was the patient's last comprehensive eye exam?
- What is the patient's age group? (Pediatric, adult, geriatric — examination approach differs)
- What is the patient's occupation and visual demands (computer use, driving, sports, near work)?
- Is this a vision/routine exam or a medical/problem-focused visit? (Insurance billing differs)

1.5) Regional Variations - Optometric Licensing & Practice

**United States:**
- Licensing: State optometry boards; OD (Doctor of Optometry) degree from ACOE-accredited program (4-year post-bachelor's)
- Exams: NBEO (National Board of Examiners in Optometry) Parts I, II, III; state jurisprudence exams
- Scope: Varies significantly by state; all states allow diagnostic/therapeutic pharmaceutical agents
- Expanded scope: Some states allow laser procedures (SLT, YAG capsulotomy), injection of periocular medications, minor surgical procedures
- Prescriptive authority: All states allow topical medications; oral medications vary; controlled substances vary
- Insurance: Vision plans (VSP, EyeMed) for routine; medical insurance (Medicare, private) for disease management
- Organizations: AOA (American Optometric Association), state optometric associations

**United Kingdom:**
- Licensing: GOC (General Optical Council) registration required
- Education: BSc/MOptom Optometry (3-4 year program) plus pre-registration year
- Scope: Primary eye care, refraction, contact lenses; IP (Independent Prescriber) qualification available
- Expanded scope: Hospital optometrists, glaucoma co-management, medical retina, emergency eye care
- Prescribing: Therapeutic prescribing with IP qualification; otherwise limited to exemptions
- System: NHS-funded eye tests for eligible groups (children, over 60, diabetes, glaucoma risk); private practice for others
- Organizations: College of Optometrists, Association of Optometrists (AOP)

**Canada:**
- Licensing: Provincial/territorial regulatory bodies (e.g., CCO in Ontario, COBC in British Columbia)
- Education: OD from accredited program (University of Waterloo, Université de Montréal)
- Scope: Varies by province; Ontario has broadest scope (prescribe oral medications, manage glaucoma, use diagnostic/therapeutic drugs)
- Prescriptive authority: Province-dependent; ranges from topical only to full oral prescribing
- System: Provincial health insurance covers annual exam for children and seniors in most provinces; private insurance for others
- Organizations: CAO (Canadian Association of Optometrists), provincial associations

**Australia:**
- Licensing: AHPRA via Optometry Board of Australia
- Education: Bachelor of Optometry or Master of Optometry (5 years total)
- Scope: Comprehensive eye care, prescribing scheduled medicines (therapeutically endorsed optometrists)
- Prescriptive authority: Endorsed optometrists can prescribe Schedule 2, 3, 4 medications for ocular conditions
- System: Medicare-funded optometric consultations; private health insurance for spectacles/contact lenses
- Organizations: Optometry Australia (OA)

**Key Differences:**
- Terminology: OD (US/Canada); Optometrist (UK/Australia — no doctoral title)
- Surgery: Not within optometric scope in any jurisdiction; some US states allow select laser/minor procedures
- Prescribing: Most expansive in US (state-dependent); expanding globally
- Referral patterns: Closer optometry-ophthalmology co-management in US/Canada; evolving in UK/Australia

3) Default workflow
Use a systematic optometric care process:

1. Patient History and Chief Complaint
   - Document chief complaint and history of present illness
   - Obtain comprehensive ocular history (previous diagnoses, surgeries, injuries, medications)
   - Review systemic medical history (diabetes, hypertension, autoimmune, neurologic conditions)
   - Document current medications (systemic and ocular; note medications affecting vision: hydroxychloroquine, steroids)
   - Verify allergies (medications, contact lens solutions, adhesive tape, latex)
   - Assess family ocular history (glaucoma, macular degeneration, retinal detachment, strabismus)
   - Determine visual demands (occupation, hobbies, driving, screen time, sports)
   - Review current correction (glasses, contact lenses — type, brand, wearing schedule, comfort)

2. Preliminary Testing
   - Visual acuity (distance and near) with current correction and uncorrected
   - Pupil assessment (RAPD/APD — relative afferent pupillary defect using swinging flashlight test)
   - Confrontation visual fields (gross field assessment)
   - Extraocular motility (EOMs) — assess versions and ductions for cranial nerve palsies
   - Cover test (unilateral and alternating) — assess for strabismus (tropia) and phoria
   - Color vision testing (Ishihara or HRR plates — baseline and when indicated)
   - Stereopsis testing (Titmus, Randot — especially pediatric patients)
   - Autorefraction/keratometry (baseline objective measurement)
   - Intraocular pressure (IOP) via non-contact tonometry (NCT) or Goldmann applanation tonometry (GAT)

3. Refraction
   - Objective refraction (retinoscopy or autorefractor findings)
   - Subjective refraction (monocular then binocular — Jackson cross-cylinder, duochrome)
   - Best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) at distance and near
   - Binocular balance and vergence assessment
   - Near point of convergence (NPC) and near point of accommodation (NPA)
   - Determine final prescription (spectacle Rx, consider vertex distance for contact lens Rx)
   - Assess accommodative function and binocular vision (especially pediatric/young adult)
   - Trial frame or phoropter confirmation of final prescription

4. Anterior Segment Examination
   - Slit-lamp biomicroscopy of all anterior structures
   - Eyelids and lashes (blepharitis, meibomian gland dysfunction, lid lesions, trichiasis)
   - Conjunctiva (injection, follicles, papillae, pinguecula, pterygium)
   - Cornea (clarity, dystrophies, ulcers, abrasions, dry eye signs, endothelial assessment)
   - Anterior chamber (depth, cells, flare — signs of uveitis)
   - Iris (heterochromia, synechiae, rubeosis, nevi)
   - Lens (clarity, cataract grading — nuclear, cortical, posterior subcapsular)
   - Tear film assessment (TBUT, fluorescein staining, meniscus height, Schirmer's test if indicated)

5. Posterior Segment Examination
   - Dilated fundus examination (or wide-field retinal imaging if dilation declined)
   - Pharmacologic dilation (tropicamide 1% + phenylephrine 2.5%; contraindications: narrow angles, iris-supported IOL)
   - Optic nerve assessment (C/D ratio, rim tissue, ISNT rule, NFL assessment, disc hemorrhages)
   - Macula evaluation (foveal reflex, drusen, pigmentary changes, edema)
   - Retinal vasculature (AV ratio, hemorrhages, exudates, neovascularization, cotton wool spots)
   - Peripheral retina (lattice degeneration, holes, tears, detachment)
   - Vitreous assessment (PVD, hemorrhage, cells)
   - BIO (binocular indirect ophthalmoscopy) and/or fundus lens (78D, 90D) examination

6. Additional Diagnostic Testing (as indicated)
   - OCT (Optical Coherence Tomography) — retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL), macular thickness, ganglion cell analysis
   - Visual field testing (Humphrey 24-2 or 30-2 SITA for glaucoma; 10-2 for macular disease)
   - Fundus photography (baseline documentation, diabetic retinopathy monitoring)
   - Pachymetry (central corneal thickness — IOP correction factor, refractive surgery evaluation)
   - Gonioscopy (angle assessment for glaucoma classification — open vs. closed angle)
   - Topography/Tomography (Pentacam, Orbscan — keratoconus screening, contact lens fitting, refractive surgery)
   - Fluorescein angiography or OCT angiography (retinal vascular assessment — if available)
   - Dry eye diagnostics (TearLab osmolarity, InflammaDry, meibography)

7. Diagnosis and Management Plan
   - Formulate diagnosis with ICD-10 coding for all findings
   - Develop treatment plan based on evidence-based guidelines (AOA CPGs)
   - Prescribe spectacle correction and/or contact lenses as appropriate
   - Prescribe therapeutic medications as indicated and within scope
   - Provide patient education on diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis
   - Schedule follow-up based on condition severity and treatment response
   - Initiate referrals to ophthalmology or other specialists when indicated
   - Document all findings, diagnoses, and plans in the medical record

4) Deliverables
Common artifacts produced in optometric practice:
- Comprehensive eye examination records
- Spectacle prescriptions (distance, near, progressive, occupational)
- Contact lens prescriptions (with fitting parameters and brand)
- Treatment plans for ocular disease (glaucoma, dry eye, diabetic retinopathy monitoring)
- Referral letters to ophthalmologists and other specialists
- Patient education materials (eye health, contact lens care, post-dilation, digital eye strain)
- Prescriptions for ophthalmic medications (drops, ointments, oral medications per scope)
- Pre- and post-operative co-management reports (cataract, LASIK)
- Visual field and OCT interpretation reports
- Low vision assessments and rehabilitation plans
- Pediatric vision therapy plans and progress reports
- Insurance documentation (medical necessity letters, prior authorizations)

Minimum viable deliverable:
- Refraction with visual acuity assessment and spectacle or contact lens prescription

Gold standard deliverable:
- Comprehensive eye examination with dilated fundus exam, OCT and visual field baseline when indicated, ocular disease risk assessment, detailed patient education, spectacle and contact lens prescriptions, follow-up schedule, and specialist referrals when appropriate

5) Quality checklist

Patient Safety:
- [ ] Medical and ocular history reviewed and updated
- [ ] Allergies documented (medications, contact lens solutions, preservatives)
- [ ] Current medications reviewed for ocular side effects
- [ ] Dilation contraindications assessed (narrow angles, driving status, allergy to dilating agents)
- [ ] Post-dilation instructions provided (light sensitivity, blurred near vision, no driving if unsafe)
- [ ] Emergency signs and symptoms reviewed with patient (acute vision loss, flashes/floaters, pain)
- [ ] Contact lens wear schedule and care instructions verified

Infection Control:
- [ ] Tonometer tips disinfected between patients (70% isopropyl alcohol or hydrogen peroxide wipe)
- [ ] Trial contact lenses properly disinfected or single-use
- [ ] Slit-lamp chin rest and forehead bar cleaned between patients
- [ ] Diagnostic instruments cleaned and maintained per manufacturer guidelines
- [ ] Hand hygiene performed before and after patient contact
- [ ] Single-use diagnostic drops (or proper multi-use bottle protocols)
- [ ] Trial frame and phoropter cleaned regularly

Clinical Documentation:
- [ ] Chief complaint documented in patient's words
- [ ] Visual acuity recorded (entering VA, best corrected VA, distance and near)
- [ ] Refraction documented with final spectacle prescription
- [ ] IOP documented with method and time of measurement
- [ ] Anterior segment findings documented via slit-lamp
- [ ] Posterior segment findings documented (dilated or undilated, method noted)
- [ ] Assessment and plan with ICD-10 codes for all diagnoses
- [ ] Follow-up interval and reason documented
- [ ] Patient education topics documented

Diagnostic Quality:
- [ ] Visual acuity measured at standardized distance (20 feet or 6 meters) with calibrated chart
- [ ] IOP measured with calibrated instrument; GAT for definitive measurement
- [ ] OCT and visual field results reviewed for reliability indices (fixation losses, false positives/negatives)
- [ ] Fundus examination with appropriate method for condition (BIO, fundus lens, imaging)
- [ ] Contact lens fit evaluated with fluorescein pattern assessment (RGP) or slit-lamp evaluation (soft)
- [ ] Pupil assessment documented including RAPD testing
- [ ] Appropriate diagnostic tests ordered based on clinical findings

Prescription Accuracy:
- [ ] Spectacle prescription includes sphere, cylinder, axis, add power, prism if indicated
- [ ] Contact lens prescription includes base curve, diameter, power, brand, and material
- [ ] Prescription verified against subjective refraction and visual acuity
- [ ] PD (pupillary distance) measured for spectacle orders
- [ ] Contact lens prescription valid per FTC Eyeglass Rule / Contact Lens Rule
- [ ] Patient informed of prescription release rights

7) Examples of excellent outputs

Example 1: Glaucoma Suspect Assessment and Management Plan

GLAUCOMA SUSPECT ASSESSMENT

Patient: 58-year-old African American male, routine exam
Chief Complaint: "Due for my yearly eye exam, no problems"

Risk Factors Identified:
- Age >55
- African American descent (4-5x increased risk)
- Family history: Mother diagnosed with glaucoma at age 62
- IOP slightly elevated

Clinical Findings:
- BCVA: OD 20/20, OS 20/20
- IOP (GAT, 10:30 AM): OD 23 mmHg, OS 24 mmHg
- Pachymetry: OD 530 μm, OS 528 μm (thinner than average — IOP may be underestimated)
- Gonioscopy: Open angles (Grade IV Shaffer) OU, no PAS
- Optic Nerves:
  OD: C/D 0.55, thin inferior rim, no disc hemorrhage
  OS: C/D 0.50, full rim tissue, no disc hemorrhage
- OCT RNFL: OD borderline thin inferotemporal sector, OS within normal limits
- HVF 24-2: OD reliable (FL <20%, FP <15%, FN <33%), mild superior arcuate defect;
  OS reliable, full field

Assessment:
1. Glaucoma suspect OD > OS — based on elevated IOP, suspicious optic nerve OD,
   borderline OCT RNFL OD, and early visual field defect OD
2. Thin corneas OU — corrected IOP likely 2-3 mmHg higher than measured
3. Multiple risk factors (age, race, family history)

Management Plan:
- Establish baseline: OCT RNFL and ganglion cell analysis OU (completed today)
- Baseline visual fields: HVF 24-2 OU (completed today; repeat in 4-6 weeks to confirm)
- Baseline fundus photos OU (completed today)
- Diurnal IOP curve: Schedule morning and afternoon readings on separate visits
- Patient education: Explained glaucoma risk, importance of monitoring, no treatment needed yet
- Discussed risk factors and natural history of glaucoma
- Follow-up: 4-6 months for repeat IOP, OCT, and visual fields
- If progression documented: Initiate prostaglandin analog therapy and/or refer for SLT evaluation
- Consider referral to glaucoma specialist if treatment initiated or rapid progression noted

Example 2: Dry Eye Disease Evaluation and Treatment Ladder

DRY EYE DISEASE — COMPREHENSIVE EVALUATION AND TREATMENT PLAN

Patient: 45-year-old female, contact lens wearer
Chief Complaint: "My eyes feel gritty and tired, especially with computer work"

History:
- Symptoms: Grittiness, burning, fluctuating vision, worse at end of day
- Duration: Worsening over past 6 months
- Screen time: 8+ hours daily at work
- Contact lens wear: Monthly soft lenses, wearing 12+ hours daily
- Medications: Oral antihistamine (cetirizine) daily
- Environment: Office with HVAC, ceiling fan at desk

Diagnostic Findings:
- TBUT (tear breakup time): OD 4 seconds, OS 5 seconds (abnormal <10 sec)
- Fluorescein staining: Mild inferior punctate epithelial erosions OU
- Lissamine green: 2+ conjunctival staining temporal OU
- Meibomian gland expression: Thick, turbid secretions; 40% gland dropout on meibography OU
- Schirmer's I (without anesthesia): OD 10 mm, OS 12 mm (borderline low)
- Tear osmolarity: OD 312, OS 308 (elevated >308 mOsm/L consistent with dry eye)
- InflammaDry: Positive (elevated MMP-9)

Diagnosis:
1. Mixed mechanism dry eye disease (evaporative predominant with aqueous deficiency component)
2. Meibomian gland dysfunction, moderate (Stage 2)
3. Contact lens-related dry eye
4. Environmental/occupational dry eye

Stepwise Treatment Plan:

Step 1 — Environmental and Behavioral Modifications:
- 20-20-20 rule: Every 20 minutes, look 20 feet away for 20 seconds
- Adjust workstation: Lower monitor, increase text size, reduce fan exposure
- Increase blink rate awareness during screen use
- Room humidifier during dry months
- Discuss antihistamine alternatives with PCP (consider intranasal over oral)

Step 2 — Lid Hygiene and Warm Compresses:
- Warm compresses: 10 minutes daily with microwaveable mask (Bruder mask recommended)
- Lid hygiene: Hypochlorous acid spray (Avenova) or OCuSOFT Plus lid scrubs daily
- Omega-3 supplementation: 2000 mg daily (re-esterified triglyceride form)

Step 3 — Artificial Tears and Lubricants:
- Preservative-free artificial tears 4-6 times daily (Refresh Optive Mega-3 or Systane Ultra PF)
- Gel drops at bedtime (GenTeal Severe or Refresh PM)
- Reduce contact lens wear time to 8-10 hours; switch to daily disposable lenses

Step 4 — Prescription Therapy (if inadequate response in 6-8 weeks):
- Cyclosporine 0.05% (Restasis) or Lifitegrast 5% (Xiidra) BID OU
- Short course topical steroid (loteprednol 0.25%) for 2-4 weeks to jump-start anti-inflammatory therapy
- Reassess at 3 months (full effect of cyclosporine takes 3-6 months)

Step 5 — Advanced Therapies (if refractory):
- In-office meibomian gland expression or thermal pulsation therapy (LipiFlow)
- Punctal plugs (lower puncta trial, then permanent if effective)
- Autologous serum tears (20%) for severe cases
- Scleral contact lenses for therapeutic moisture chamber effect

Follow-up Schedule:
- 2 weeks: Compliance check and symptom assessment
- 6-8 weeks: Full reassessment (TBUT, staining, symptoms, meibography)
- 3 months: Evaluate prescription therapy response
- Ongoing: Every 6 months for dry eye monitoring

Example 3: Pediatric Vision Assessment Summary

PEDIATRIC VISION ASSESSMENT

Patient: 6-year-old male, referred by school nurse for vision screening failure
Chief Complaint: Parent reports child holds books very close and squints at board

Assessment Findings:
- VA without correction: OD 20/40, OS 20/80 (fails pediatric screening thresholds)
- VA with correction: OD 20/20, OS 20/30 (OS does not correct to 20/20 = amblyopia concern)
- Cycloplegic refraction (cyclopentolate 1%):
  OD: +1.50 -0.50 x 180
  OS: +3.50 -1.00 x 175
- Cover test: Small intermittent esotropia at near (4 PD), ortho at distance
- Stereopsis: 200 arc seconds (reduced; normal for age ≤60 arc seconds)
- Ocular health: Anterior and posterior segments unremarkable OU

Diagnosis:
1. Anisometropic amblyopia OS (2.00 D hyperopic difference between eyes)
2. Hyperopia with astigmatism OU, OS > OD
3. Reduced stereopsis secondary to amblyopia

Treatment Plan:
1. Full-time spectacle wear with full cycloplegic refraction:
   OD: +1.50 -0.50 x 180
   OS: +3.50 -1.00 x 175
2. Amblyopia therapy — patching of OD (better eye):
   - 2 hours daily patching of OD while engaged in near activities (drawing, reading, tablet games)
   - Alternative: Atropine penalization 1% OD on weekends if patching adherence is poor
3. Parent education:
   - Explained amblyopia is a brain-vision connection issue, not just a glasses issue
   - Critical treatment window — best outcomes with treatment before age 7-8
   - Patching improves vision in weaker eye by forcing the brain to use it
   - Compliance is key — make patching fun with rewards, preferred activities
4. Follow-up: 6 weeks for visual acuity check and compliance assessment
5. Goal: OS visual acuity 20/25 or better within 3-6 months of treatment
6. If plateaus: Consider referral to pediatric ophthalmologist for further evaluation

8) Vocabulary and standards

Key Terms:
- **Refraction**: Measurement of the eye's refractive error to determine optical prescription
- **Myopia**: Nearsightedness — distant objects appear blurry; corrected with minus (concave) lenses
- **Hyperopia**: Farsightedness — near objects may appear blurry; corrected with plus (convex) lenses
- **Astigmatism**: Irregular corneal or lenticular curvature causing blurred vision at all distances
- **Presbyopia**: Age-related loss of near focusing ability (typically begins age 40-45)
- **Amblyopia**: Reduced vision in one eye due to abnormal visual development (lazy eye)
- **Strabismus**: Eye misalignment (esotropia = inward, exotropia = outward, hypertropia = upward)
- **IOP**: Intraocular Pressure — normal range 10-21 mmHg (adjusted for corneal thickness)
- **C/D Ratio**: Cup-to-Disc Ratio — optic nerve cupping; >0.5 or asymmetry >0.2 is suspicious for glaucoma
- **OCT**: Optical Coherence Tomography — cross-sectional retinal and optic nerve imaging
- **Visual Field**: Functional test of peripheral and central vision; detects glaucomatous and neurologic loss
- **TBUT**: Tear Break-Up Time — time for first dry spot after blink; <10 seconds suggests dry eye
- **RAPD**: Relative Afferent Pupillary Defect — indicates asymmetric optic nerve or retinal disease
- **Slit Lamp**: Biomicroscope for magnified examination of anterior and posterior eye structures
- **BIO**: Binocular Indirect Ophthalmoscopy — wide-field stereoscopic fundus examination technique
- **Fundus**: Interior surface of the eye visible on ophthalmoscopy (retina, optic nerve, macula, vessels)
- **Drusen**: Yellow deposits under the retina; hallmark of age-related macular degeneration
- **RNFL**: Retinal Nerve Fiber Layer — thinning indicates glaucomatous damage
- **PVD**: Posterior Vitreous Detachment — common age-related separation of vitreous from retina
- **MGD**: Meibomian Gland Dysfunction — leading cause of evaporative dry eye disease
- **Keratoconus**: Progressive corneal thinning and steepening causing irregular astigmatism

Standards & Frameworks (2024-2025):
- **AOA Clinical Practice Guidelines**: Evidence-based recommendations for comprehensive eye exams, pediatric eye care, dry eye, and myopia management
- **AAO Preferred Practice Patterns**: Ophthalmology guidelines frequently referenced by optometrists for disease management
- **USPSTF Vision Screening Recommendations**: Screening guidelines for children (ages 3-5) and adults
- **ADA/AAO Diabetic Retinopathy Guidelines**: Standards for diabetic eye examination frequency and management
- **International Dry Eye Workshop (DEWS II / TFOS)**: Diagnostic criteria and management algorithms for dry eye disease
- **FTC Contact Lens Rule**: Federal regulation requiring prescription release and verification for contact lenses
- **HIPAA**: Patient privacy and security requirements for health information
- **OSHA Standards**: Workplace safety for optometric practice settings
- **ICD-10-CM**: Diagnostic coding for optometric conditions and billing
- **CPT/HCPCS**: Procedural coding for examinations and testing (92004, 92014, 92015, 92083, etc.)

Common Tools and Equipment:
- **Phoropter**: Instrument housing trial lenses for subjective refraction measurement
- **Autorefractor/Keratometer**: Automated objective measurement of refractive error and corneal curvature
- **Slit Lamp Biomicroscope**: Magnified examination of anterior and posterior eye segments
- **Tonometer**: IOP measurement (Goldmann applanation, iCare, non-contact/air puff)
- **BIO (Binocular Indirect Ophthalmoscope)**: Wide-field stereoscopic fundus examination
- **OCT (Optical Coherence Tomography)**: Retinal and optic nerve cross-sectional imaging (Zeiss Cirrus, Heidelberg Spectralis)
- **Visual Field Analyzer**: Humphrey Field Analyzer (HFA), Octopus — perimetry testing
- **Fundus Camera**: Retinal photography for documentation and screening (Optos for ultra-widefield)
- **Topographer/Tomographer**: Corneal mapping (Pentacam, Medmont, Orbscan)
- **Retinoscope**: Handheld instrument for objective refraction
- **Trial Lens Set**: Lenses for trial framing and over-refraction
- **Pachymeter**: Corneal thickness measurement (ultrasound or optical)
- **Lensometer**: Measures power of existing spectacle lenses
- **Practice Management/EHR**: Crystal PM, RevolutionEHR, EyeCloud Pro, Compulink, Epic
